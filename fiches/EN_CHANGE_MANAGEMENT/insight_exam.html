Q1
Leadership & Organizational Identity
10 pts
How did leadership and organizational identity shape the process of embedding sustainability into the core strategy of a growing company such as Phoenix?
🔵 Theory to Mobilize
  • Transformational Leadership (Burns 1978, Bass 1985) — Leaders inspire followers to transcend self-interest for a collective purpose. 4I model: Idealized Influence, Inspirational Motivation, Intellectual Stimulation, Individualized Consideration
  • VRIO (Barney 1991) — Identity-driven sustainability is Valuable (premium pricing), Rare (cost-driven competitors), Inimitable (culturally embedded, cannot be copied), Organized (Natural Step, director) → durable competitive advantage
  • Carroll's Pyramid (1991) — Phoenix operates at all 4 levels simultaneously; ethical/philanthropic decisions shape economic ones (≠ standard firm where economic always wins)
  • Kotter's 8 Steps — Phoenix embodied steps 1–3 from day 1: urgency (health & environment), coalition (founding trio), vision (organic, authentic NZ)
  • Paradox theory (Smith & Lewis 2011) — Both/and: profit AND sustainability held in tension rather than choosing one
🟢 Phoenix Evidence
  • Morrison's identity-driven decisions: refused glass→plastic (Idealized Influence), refused bottled water (ethical over economic)
  • The Natural Step framework + sustainability director = Intellectual Stimulation at org level
  • Staff trips, social culture → Individualized Consideration → identity internalized, low turnover
  • "Squeaky clean" standard = accountability mechanism for the leader himself
  • VRIO in practice: identity cannot be copied by Coca-Cola or Frucor (anchored in founder history, culture, community)
🟡 Critical Reflection (what separates a 9–10 from a 7–8)
  • Identity is both an enabler (authentic, hard to imitate) and a constraint (limits scaling, rejects profitable options)
  • Morrison's reluctance to bring outside investors = identity protection vs financial vulnerability — can the company survive growth?
  • Contrast with Kotter: his model assumes change is triggered by crisis. Phoenix's embedding was proactive, from founding — identity-led change, not crisis-led
  • Risk: if Morrison exits, can identity outlive its founder? (succession = unresolved tension)
✏ Model Answer Structure (10–12 lines)

Phoenix illustrates how transformational leadership (Bass, 1985) can embed sustainability as organizational identity rather than CSR add-on. Morrison's 4I approach — refusing plastic and water (Idealized Influence), the "squeaky clean" vision (Inspirational Motivation), adopting The Natural Step (Intellectual Stimulation), staff trips (Individualized Consideration) — ensured values permeated the firm. This identity constitutes a VRIO resource (Barney, 1991): Valuable (organic premium), Rare (cost-driven industry), Inimitable (culturally rooted), Organized (sustainability director). Unlike Carroll's standard model where economic logic dominates, Phoenix operated at the ethical and philanthropic levels first, letting them constrain economic decisions. Smith & Lewis's (2011) paradox theory explains this "both/and" stance — profit and sustainability held in productive tension. Critically, this was not a Kotter-style crisis-driven change but an identity-led process from founding, which is both its strength (hard to copy) and its risk: Morrison's reluctance to accept outside investors suggests identity may be a fragile asset dependent on founder continuity.

Q2
Rigidities & Resistance to Change — Creative Responses
10 pts
As the industry was dominated by cost-driven giants, what types of rigidities and resistance to change did Phoenix encounter, and how might it creatively address them?
🔵 Theory to Mobilize
  • Institutional Isomorphism (DiMaggio & Powell 1983) — 3 mechanisms: Coercive (supermarkets demanding 1L bottles/multipacks), Mimetic (pressure to behave like Coca-Cola), Normative (industry norms of plastic, mass production, low cost)
  • Lewin's Force Field Analysis — Driving forces (organic consumer awareness, café culture, health trends) vs Restraining forces (cost dominance, "organic ghettos", supermarket power, financial constraints)
  • Resistance to Change (Kotter & Schlesinger) — Self-interest (retailers protect margins), Misunderstanding (organics = niche = not mainstream), Different valuation of sustainability, Low tolerance for disruption
  • Paradox theory (Smith & Lewis 2011) — Industry stuck in "either/or" (cheap OR sustainable); Phoenix's creative response is the "both/and" model
  • VRIO — Phoenix's scarcity of resources forces creative solutions (refrigerator deal, café focus)
🟢 Phoenix Evidence — Rigidities Encountered
  • Coercive: Supermarkets demanding 1L bottles, multipacks → forced standardization that commoditizes Phoenix
  • Mimetic: Coca-Cola & Frucor investing in juices, organic categories → competitive encroachment
  • Normative: "Organic ghettos" in supermarkets = physical institutionalization of organic as marginal
  • Organic ingredients 2× price + seasonality = supply chain rigidity
  • San Pellegrino encroaching on café channel → even the safe alternative channel threatened
  • Tight cash flow → limits Phoenix's capacity to fight rigidities (no advertising budget)
🟢 Phoenix Evidence — Creative Responses
  • Café channel strategy = creative bypass of mainstream supermarket power → direct access to consumers valuing premium organic
  • Eye-level shelf negotiation (fridges in exchange for visibility) = resource-constrained creativity
  • Long-term supplier relationships = reduce supply volatility, build loyalty beyond market pricing
  • Focus on beverages only after jams/salsa failure = strategic concentration as creative response to resource limits
  • GE-free NZ advocacy = shaping external environment rather than just adapting to it → reduce coercive pressure at industry level
🟡 Critical Reflection
  • The creative responses are mostly avoidance strategies (bypass supermarkets via cafés) rather than transformation strategies — Phoenix shapes the fringes but hasn't transformed the mainstream
  • Lewin's Refreeze is the unsolved problem: Phoenix can unfreeze norms in cafés but the industry (supermarkets, giants) remains frozen
  • Risk: Café channel becoming commoditized (San Pellegrino) means the "escape route" from mainstream rigidities is closing
✏ Model Answer Structure (10–12 lines)

Phoenix faced three forms of institutional isomorphism (DiMaggio & Powell, 1983): coercive pressure from supermarkets demanding 1L bottles and multipacks, mimetic pressure from Coca-Cola entering the organic segment, and normative pressure through "organic ghettos" that institutionalized organic as marginal. Using Lewin's Force Field Analysis, restraining forces (cost dominance, financial constraints, supply chain rigidity) outweigh driving forces (health trends, café culture) in mainstream channels. Resistance (Kotter & Schlesinger) takes the form of self-interest (retailers protecting margins) and institutional misunderstanding of organic as niche. Phoenix's creative responses exploit the paradox (Smith & Lewis, 2011): rather than fighting mainstream rigidities head-on, it bypassed them by building the café channel — negotiating eye-level space in exchange for fridges (a resource-constrained innovation), and building long-term supplier relationships to reduce supply chain volatility. Critically, these remain avoidance strategies rather than systemic transformation: Phoenix thrives in the fringes but has not yet defrosted the mainstream. With San Pellegrino encroaching on the café channel, even this creative escape route is narrowing.

Q3
Phoenix as Multiplier Firm — Systemic Change
10 pts
How could Phoenix act as a multiplier firm to create broader systemic change in the beverage sector while safeguarding its own values?
🔵 Theory to Mobilize
  • Multiplier Firm concept — companies that influence their broader ecosystem (supply chains, competitors, regulators, consumers) beyond their own operations
  • Stakeholder theory (Freeman 1984) — obligations to all stakeholders = platform for influence: suppliers, consumers, employees, regulators, community
  • Shared Value (Porter & Kramer 2011) — creating economic value AND societal value = scalable model that others can learn from or adopt
  • Institutional entrepreneurship (DiMaggio 1988) — firms with enough legitimacy can reshape industry norms, not just adapt to them
  • VRIO paradox — if Phoenix's practices become industry standard, they lose rarity → paradox of being too successful as a multiplier
  • Carroll's Pyramid — systemic change requires going beyond philanthropic to structural advocacy
🟢 Phoenix Evidence — Multiplier Actions Already Taken
  • GE-free NZ campaign = active reshaping of regulatory environment → institutional entrepreneurship
  • Awards for sustainable business = external credibility that normalizes sustainability as viable business model
  • Café channel = normalized premium organic in everyday consumption, creating consumer demand that influences competitors
  • Supplier relationships = supporting small organic farmers → multiplier effect on supply side
  • Refused bottled water = public advocacy that raises accountability standards for the whole industry
🟢 How to Scale Without Compromising Values
  • Coalition of like-minded firms (B Corp model) → collective influence without individual compromise
  • Shared Value approach: demonstrate to the industry that organic + premium = viable profitable model
  • Selective outside investors aligned with mission (impact investors) to fund multiplier activities
  • Remain authentic: only advocate externally what you practice internally — "squeaky clean" test becomes more scrutinized as influence grows
🟡 Critical Reflection — The Core Paradox
  • The more Phoenix succeeds as a multiplier, the more competitors copy it → VRIO advantage eroded (sustainability becomes industry standard = no longer rare)
  • Morrison's reluctance to accept outside investors limits multiplier capacity: you cannot change a sector from a position of financial fragility
  • Institutional entrepreneurship requires legitimacy AND resources — Phoenix has legitimacy but lacks scale to reshape mainstream norms
✏ Model Answer Structure (10–12 lines)

Phoenix already exhibits multiplier behaviors: the GE-free NZ campaign and bottled water refusal demonstrate institutional entrepreneurship (DiMaggio, 1988) — actively reshaping norms rather than adapting to them. Through stakeholder theory (Freeman, 1984), Phoenix's long-term supplier relationships create a multiplier effect at the supply level, normalizing organic sourcing economics. Its café channel strategy generated a new consumer segment that now pressures competitors to respond, embodying Shared Value (Porter & Kramer, 2011). To scale systemic impact while protecting values, Phoenix could form a coalition of like-minded firms (B Corp model) to achieve collective institutional influence no single company can reach alone. However, a core paradox emerges: the more Phoenix diffuses its practices, the less Rare and Inimitable they become (VRIO, Barney 1991) — sustainability becomes table stakes, not competitive advantage. Critically, institutional entrepreneurship requires both legitimacy (Phoenix has) and resources (Phoenix lacks) — Morrison's reluctance to bring outside investors leaves the firm too financially fragile to lead sector-level change at scale.

Q4
Measuring & Communicating Sustainability — Avoiding Greenwashing
10 pts
How could Phoenix measure and communicate the impact of its sustainability practices in a way that reinforces credibility and competitive advantage, while avoiding the risk of overselling?
🔵 Theory to Mobilize
  • Triple Bottom Line (Elkington 1994) — measure and report People (labor conditions, staff wellbeing, supplier fairness), Planet (GHG, waste, organic %), Profit (sustainable margins, CLV) — all three transparently
  • GHG Accounting (Scope 1/2/3) — credibility requires measuring full value chain impact, especially Scope 3 (ingredients, transport, distribution)
  • Signaling theory — costly signals (refusing profitable options = plastic, water) are far more credible than cheap talk (labels, slogans)
  • Carroll's Pyramid — Ethical responsibility goes beyond legal compliance; authentic communication = acting beyond what is required, not just reporting what is required
  • VRIO — credible, third-party verified measurement makes sustainability claims hard to imitate → competitive advantage
  • Greenwashing (Carroll) — claiming more than you deliver; Legal ≠ Ethical; risk is particularly acute when growing and seeking investors
🟢 Phoenix Evidence — What Already Works
  • Costly signals: refusing glass→plastic and refusing water = most credible communication (sacrifice = proof)
  • The Natural Step framework = science-based (not marketing-driven) measurement system
  • Awards for sustainable business = external validation / third-party signal
  • Sustainability director = formal accountability structure
  • GE-free NZ advocacy = public commitment that raises Phoenix's own accountability standard
  • Morrison's "squeaky clean" standard = self-aware risk management against hypocrisy
🟢 Recommendations to Improve Without Overselling
  • Measure Scope 3 (ingredient sourcing, transport) — the hardest part, but most credible if reported honestly
  • Triple Bottom Line reporting: quantify People (staff turnover, supplier fair prices), Planet (organic %, GHG), Profit (margin per product)
  • Admit trade-offs honestly: glass → higher transport emissions vs. plastic → acknowledge complexity
  • Third-party certification (organic, B Corp) rather than self-declaration
  • Communicate the WHY (ethical reasoning, refused water) not just the WHAT (organic label) → audience understands intent, not just attribute
🟡 Critical Reflection
  • Phoenix's current communication is implicit (actions speak) rather than explicit (reports, metrics) — this is credible but not scalable: as the company grows and seeks investors, stakeholders will require formal TBL reporting
  • Key tension: authentic → formal reporting risks becoming performative (the act of reporting replaces the act of doing)
  • CSRD (EU directive) is not yet applicable to NZ, but any expansion to European markets will require it — early adoption could be a competitive advantage
✏ Model Answer Structure (10–12 lines)

Phoenix's most credible sustainability communication already relies on costly signals (signaling theory): refusing glass→plastic and declining to bottle water sacrifice commercial gains to prove genuine commitment — more credible than any label or slogan. To formalize this advantage, Phoenix should adopt Triple Bottom Line reporting (Elkington, 1994) — measuring People (staff wellbeing, supplier fair prices), Planet (organic %, GHG scope 1/2/3), and Profit (sustainable margins) — and publish it transparently, including trade-offs (glass = higher transport emissions). The Natural Step framework already provides a science-based measurement foundation; third-party certification (B Corp) would externalize verification and reduce greenwashing risk. VRIO analysis (Barney, 1991) shows that credible, independently verified measurement is itself Inimitable — generic organic brands cannot replicate Phoenix's history of principled sacrifices. Critically, Carroll's ethical level requires admitting constraints, not just showcasing achievements: honest reporting of the glass/transport trade-off or limited Scope 3 data is more credible than a polished sustainability report. The risk, however, is that formalizing reporting transforms authentic behavior into performative compliance — the very culture of "squeaky clean" integrity may be harder to preserve under investor and regulatory scrutiny.
