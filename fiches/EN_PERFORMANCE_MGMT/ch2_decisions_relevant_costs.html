Performance Management • Key concepts, decision trees & flash questions

Relevant vs Irrelevant Costs

3 conditions for a cost to be relevant: (1) Relates to the objective, (2) Is a future cost, (3) Varies with the decision.
Relevant CostsIrrelevant Costs
Opportunity costsSunk (past) costs
Differential future outlay costsCommitted costs (irrevocable contracts)
Non-differential future costs
Opportunity cost: Value of the next best alternative forgone. No cash outlay, but represents lost potential wealth.

Decision Framework

Steps: (1) Identify all costs → (2) Eliminate irrelevant (sunk, committed, non-differential) → (3) Determine opportunity costs → (4) Calculate differential outlay costs → (5) Sum relevant costs = minimum price → (6) Consider non-quantifiable factors

Non-Measurable Costs & Benefits

  • Loss of reputation, customer dissatisfaction
  • Quality risks from casual labour
  • Uncertain demand, morale effects
  • Linked revenues (closing one route may affect others)

Flash Questions

Q: A car was bought for £9,000 and can now be sold for £10,500. What is the relevant cost of keeping it?
A: £10,500 (opportunity cost). The £9,000 is a sunk cost.
Q: What is the relevant cost of labour when workers have spare capacity?
A: £0 — they are paid regardless.
Q: Material is in stock, not used for anything else, and can be sold for £23/unit. What is the relevant cost?
A: £23/unit (net realisable value = opportunity cost).
Q: Material is in constant use by the business. What is the relevant cost?
A: Current replacement (buying-in) cost, because using it means it must be replaced.
Q: A worker is redeployed from a job that generates £60/hour. What is the relevant labour cost?
A: £60/hour (opportunity cost of the revenue forgone).
Q: What is a committed cost?
A: A future cost arising from an irrevocable decision (e.g. a binding contract) — it is irrelevant because it must be paid regardless.
