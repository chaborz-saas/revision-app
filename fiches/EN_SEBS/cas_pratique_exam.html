PART 1 — The 7 Questions (4 pts each)
Question 1 / 7
What are the potential limitations of a sustainable and ethical business strategy? What advantages can a company gain from such a strategy?
  • LIMITATIONS / RISKS
  • High short-term transition costs (ESG investments, CSRD reporting, industrial renovation)
  • Risk of greenwashing: gap between communication and reality = loss of trust + legal disputes
  • Asymmetric competitiveness: non-EU competitors not subject to the same constraints (e.g. Made in China 2025 vs Green Deal)
  • Difficulties measuring and standardising ESG indicators / double materiality
  • Risk of rebound effect (Jevons Paradox): increased efficiency → higher overall consumption → emissions don't fall
  • Internal resistance to change (employees, short-term shareholders)
  • Limits of voluntary carbon markets: carbon price insufficient to trigger transition
  • ADVANTAGES / OPPORTUNITIES
  • Access to green finance (EU Taxonomy, green bonds, ESG funds) → reduced cost of capital
  • Regulatory anticipation (CSRD, CBAM, taxonomy) = lower exposure to future penalties
  • Talent attractiveness: employees and consumers prefer committed companies
  • Reduction of physical and transition risks Mark Carney: physical risks, transition risks, liability risks
  • Innovation and new markets (circular economy, green hydrogen, bioeconomy)
  • Enhanced legitimacy with stakeholders → better licence to operate
  • Alignment with SDGs (UN) → international competitive differentiation
  • KEY CONCEPT to mention: Triple Bottom Line John Elkington — People, Planet, Profit
Question 2 / 7
There is a constrained physical framework — can you explain the consequences for the business strategies of organisations?
  • THE CONSTRAINED PHYSICAL FRAMEWORK = PLANETARY BOUNDARIES Johan Rockström
  • 9 planetary boundaries defining a "safe operating space" for humanity
  • 6/9 boundaries already crossed (2023): climate, biodiversity, nitrogen/phosphorus cycles, freshwater, land use, novel entities
  • Concept of Anthropocene Paul Crutzen: human activity has become a geological force
  • STRATEGIC CONSEQUENCES FOR COMPANIES
  • Physical constraint on resources → end of the assumption of unlimited resources (oil, critical metals, water)
  • Risk of tipping points: irreversibility = impossibility of late adaptation
  • Double carbon constraint Gaël Giraud: fossil scarcity + CO2 accumulation → fossil-based business models not viable
  • Risk of stranded assets: fossil reserves, carbon-intensive equipment lose their value
  • Doughnut Economics Kate Raworth: goal = stay between the social foundation and the ecological ceiling
  • Obligation to rethink value chains: sourcing, production, logistics within planetary limits
  • CSRD reporting = legal obligation to measure and disclose impacts on planetary boundaries
  • EXAMPLE: Amsterdam has adopted the Doughnut model to guide its urban development
Question 3 / 7
The International Energy Agency publishes a report every year — what recommendations would you give to a Board of Directors (COMEX) based on the 2024 report?
  • IEA 2024 REPORT — KEY MESSAGES (World Energy Outlook 2024)
  • Global fossil demand peak expected before 2030 (coal already peaked, oil/gas soon)
  • Clean energy investments exceed fossil investments for the first time (clean energy > $2 trillion/year)
  • Current trajectory leads to +2.4°C — insufficient for Paris Agreement goals
  • Explosive growth of solar PV and EVs in China and emerging markets
  • Critical challenges: energy storage, green hydrogen, smart grids
  • RECOMMENDATIONS TO THE BOARD
  • Map the company's fossil energy exposure → identify assets at risk of stranding
  • Accelerate operational energy efficiency ahead of upcoming regulatory constraints (CBAM, ETS)
  • Integrate an internal carbon price into investment decisions (recommended by the IEA)
  • Secure supply of critical minerals (lithium, cobalt, rare earths) via long-term contracts or vertical integration
  • Anticipate Climate Minsky Moment risk Hyman Minsky / Mark Carney: sudden repricing of carbon-intensive assets = systemic financial risk
  • Seize opportunities in hard-to-abate decarbonisation (green hydrogen, electrification of heavy industry)
  • ANALYTICAL FRAMEWORK: Physical + transition + liability risks Mark Carney
Question 4 / 7
Economists have long proposed definitions and explanations for GDP growth. What are the links between the definition of economic growth and a sustainable business strategy?
  • CLASSICAL DEFINITIONS OF GROWTH
  • GDP = measure of production → associates progress with increased production and consumption flows
  • Environmental Kuznets Curve Simon Kuznets: pollution rises then falls with growth → challenged since CO2 continues to increase
  • Decoupling hypothesis (growth without emissions): absolute decoupling not demonstrated at the global scale
  • TENSIONS BETWEEN CLASSICAL GROWTH AND SUSTAINABILITY
  • Jevons Paradox / Rebound Effect William Stanley Jevons: efficiency gains → higher overall consumption → emissions don't fall
  • GDP growth based on finite resource consumption = incompatible with planetary boundaries
  • Prosperity without Growth Tim Jackson: wellbeing does not require infinite material growth
  • TOWARDS A REDEFINITION OF PERFORMANCE
  • Triple Bottom Line John Elkington: People, Planet, Profit → integrate social and environmental dimensions
  • Doughnut Economics Kate Raworth: growth within planetary limits and above the social foundation
  • Alternative indicators: HDI, subjective wellbeing, ecological footprint (beyond GDP alone)
  • Sustainable strategy = decouple value creation from resource consumption → circular economy, servitisation
  • STRATEGIC LINK: A sustainable company redefines its value creation beyond profit to embed sustainability as a lasting competitive advantage
Question 5 / 7
What are the major current public policies in the context of sustainable and ethical business strategies?
  • 🇪🇺 EUROPEAN UNION — Green Deal & regulation
  • European Green Deal (2019): carbon neutrality 2050, -55% emissions by 2030 (Fit for 55)
  • CSRD (Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive): mandatory double materiality reporting for thousands of companies
  • EU Green Taxonomy: classification of sustainable economic activities → guides capital allocation
  • CBAM (Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism): carbon tariff on imports → protects EU industrial competitiveness
  • ETS (Emissions Trading Scheme): market carbon price → incentive to decarbonise
  • 🇺🇸 UNITED STATES — Inflation Reduction Act (IRA 2022)
  • $370 billion in subsidies and tax credits for the US energy transition
  • Goal: reshore green production in the USA → risk of diverting investments away from Europe
  • Regulatory competition effect (competition for green investment)
  • 🇨🇳 CHINA — Made in China 2025 & green dominance
  • Massive industrial strategy: dominate global green technologies (EVs, solar, batteries)
  • BYD, CATL = global leadership → challenge for European industries
  • Critical dependencies: rare earths, batteries → industrial sovereignty at stake
  • GEOPOLITICAL TENSIONS → STRATEGIC CONSEQUENCES
  • Risk of fragmentation (EU/USA decoupling vs China) → rethink supply chains
  • Opportunities: IRA subsidies, European funds (Just Transition Fund, InvestEU)
  • Obligation to integrate technological sovereignty into corporate strategy
Question 6 / 7 — ⭐ Appeared in exam (highlighted)
What is the double carbon constraint? What are the consequences for the economic world?
  • DEFINITION — Double Carbon Constraint Gaël Giraud / Jean-Marc Jancovici
  • Constraint 1 — Depletion of fossil resources: oil, coal, gas are finite resources → increasing extraction costs → production peak
  • Constraint 2 — Accumulation of CO2 emissions: burning remaining reserves = massively breaching climate limits → collapse of natural systems
  • Both constraints act simultaneously: we can neither continue burning fossil fuels (climate constraint) nor stop abruptly (economic dependency constraint)
  • ECONOMIC CONSEQUENCES
  • Stranded assets: unexploitable fossil reserves → major oil company balance sheets need massive revaluation
  • Risk of Climate Minsky Moment inspired by Hyman Minsky: sudden and brutal repricing of all carbon-intensive assets → systemic financial crisis
  • Challenge to the fossil-dependent growth model: cheap and abundant energy = false assumption
  • Energy price volatility → uncertainty for long-term industrial investments
  • Acceleration of forced energy transition: not only by choice but by physical necessity
  • Mandatory divestment from the fossil sector → reallocation to renewables, storage, efficiency
  • FOR COMPANIES:
  • Map fossil energy exposure
  • Plan value chain decarbonisation before being forced to
  • Integrate the double constraint into 5–10 year strategic scenarios
  • KEY FIGURE: Current trajectory = +2.4 to +3°C. Remaining carbon budget compatible with +1.5°C = limited to ~500 GtCO2 (IPCC)
Question 7 / 7
How does the concept of the circular economy modify the business strategies of companies?
  • DEFINITION — Circular Economy
  • An economic model aimed at eliminating waste and keeping materials and products in circulation for as long as possible
  • Opposed to the linear model: "extract → make → dispose" (take-make-dispose)
  • 3 principles (Ellen MacArthur Foundation): eliminate waste, keep products/materials in use, regenerate natural systems
  • CHANGES TO BUSINESS STRATEGIES
  • Business model shift: from product sales → selling usage/service (Product as a Service, leasing, pay-per-use)
  • Mandatory eco-design: design products for repairability, disassembly, recyclability from the outset
  • Integration of the reverse value chain (reverse logistics): collection, refurbishment, recycling of end-of-life products
  • Reduced dependence on virgin raw materials → resilience in the face of critical mineral supply tensions
  • New value sources: sale of recycled secondary materials, maintenance services, remanufacturing
  • Regulatory requirements: EU Ecodesign Regulation, repairability index, take-back obligations → circularity becomes compliance
  • COMPANY EXAMPLES
  • IKEA: developing circular furniture lines (rental, take-back, repair)
  • Renault: Flins plant (Re-Factory) entirely dedicated to circular economy in automotive
  • Michelin: selling per kilometre driven (not per tyre) → Product as a Service model
  • LINK with double carbon constraint: circularity = partial response to physical resource scarcity

Case Study 2 — Strategic memo to the Board of Directors
You are Head of Innovation and Strategy. The Board asks you for a memo on the current challenges of the business model and public policies (Green Deal, ecological planning…) with your recommendations for a positive impact. Quality of reasoning + sourced examples required.
  • MEMO STRUCTURE:
  • Header: Memo to the Board of Directors — Subject: Sustainable strategic challenges and recommendations
  • 1. Diagnosis — The constrained context
  • 6/9 planetary boundaries crossed → finite resources, real climate urgency
  • Double carbon constraint → end of fossil-dependent growth
  • Current trajectory: +2.4°C → physical risks (floods, droughts) + regulatory risks
  • 2. Regulatory framework — Constraints and opportunities
  • EU Green Deal: -55% by 2030, neutrality by 2050 → unavoidable legal constraint
  • CSRD mandatory → double materiality reporting from 2024–2025 depending on company size
  • CBAM → protects our competitiveness vs non-carbon-taxed Asian competitors
  • US IRA → risk of green investment relocation to the USA
  • Made in China 2025 → pressure on EVs, batteries, solar → dependencies to manage
  • 3. Strategic recommendations
  • Short term: map carbon footprint (Scopes 1, 2, 3) and energy exposure
  • Short term: prepare CSRD compliance (double materiality, ESG KPIs)
  • Medium term: integrate an internal carbon price into investment decisions
  • Medium term: secure critical mineral supply (diversification, recycling)
  • Long term: reorient business model towards circular economy and servitisation
  • Long term: align strategy with SDGs and Doughnut Economics framework
  • 4. Conclusion
  • Sustainability is no longer an ethical choice: it is a strategic and financial imperative
  • Companies ahead of the transition will create tomorrow's lasting competitive advantages
