Summary — Change Management Ch 1: The Standard Economy and the Environment

Introduction: Course Origins and the Need for Transition

The Observation: Exponential Progress

Over the past 50 years, human health has improved more than in the millions of previous years, and this improvement applies to everyone on the planet. Global GDP has increased dramatically:

  • 1944: approximately $8 trillion
  • 2000: approximately $60 trillion
  • 2022: exceeded $100 trillion; 2024: around $111.3 trillion

Economic growth is essentially a recent phenomenon, concentrated in the last two centuries.

The Flip Side of the Coin

Environmental destruction: We have degraded three-quarters of land and two-thirds of seas and oceans, harming our own wellbeing, that of our descendants, and that of other species.

Societies have become increasingly inequitable, fragmented, and polarized over the past 40 years. A 2025 study published in Nature Climate Change reveals that the richest 10% of the planet are responsible for two-thirds of the global warming observed since 1990 (Schöngart et al., 2025). The study highlights the importance of emissions linked to financial investments, not just personal consumption.

Era of "bad-growth": Our economic systems have become self-destructive — an economy that produces in excess, depletes the planet's biocapacity, overexploits resources, hinders regeneration, and pulls future income into the present. It is an economy of presentism, excess, and precarity.

The Responsibility of Economic Sciences

Mainstream economics has fixated on three obsessions: efficiency, profitability, and growth. Of 77,000 articles published in the 9 most influential economics journals since their inception to 2019, only 57 were devoted to climate change — less than 0.1%.

In 2019, Nicholas Stern and Andrew Oswald concluded that "economists are letting the world down." Traditional tools favored by economists have failed to change the situation: greenhouse gas emissions continue to rise, species extinctions are accelerating, and pressure on planetary boundaries keeps intensifying.

A Necessary Transition

Transition (Latin transitio): Means "passage." It has a character that is both gradual and profound. It is not a simple adjustment, nor a sudden revolution. In chemistry, it denotes the change from one equilibrium state to another. The shift toward a new economy implies political and social innovations.

The course objective is to propose a critical vision of economics, examining the discipline from social, ecological, and philosophical perspectives, asking whether economics itself needs to undergo a transition.

I. In the Beginning: Economics as the Science of Moderation

A. The First Definitions

Economic analysis has been concerned with justice and ecological issues from its very origins. Economics was invented by Xenophon and Aristotle approximately 2,500 years ago.

Oikonomia (Economy): From Greek oikos (home, hearth) + nomos (law, rules of management). Aristotle defined economics as the discipline of moderation in the service of satisfying basic human needs — the internal management of a home or family.

From the outset, economics is the discipline that reconciles human needs with the constraints of their environment, seeking balance between reasonable needs and limited resources. The primary goal of economists is to seek a social optimum: how to best satisfy human needs when resources are limited.

Economists disagree on explanations (microeconomics vs. macroeconomics) and on the policies to implement. Yet despite disagreements, early thinkers agreed on one point: the economy must be based on moral values — fair prices, condemnation of usury and greed. A "good economy" is one focused on the community.

Historical shift: Over the centuries, the rise of individualism pushed the search for social optimum to the background, leaving only the notion of individual optimum.

B. Nature as the Source of Power: The Physiocrats

Appearing in France around 1750, the physiocrat movement included philosophers and politicians such as François Quesnay (physician to King Louis XV), Turgot, Dupont de Nemours, and Mirabeau. Physiocracy was a reaction to Colbertism and the mercantilist doctrine.

Physiocracy: From Greek physis (nature) + kratos (power). Literally: "government by nature" or "power of nature." Physiocrats believe the economy obeys natural, just, immutable and universal laws, and that the state must not intervene in economic mechanisms.

Key ideas of the physiocrats:

  • Wealth comes from the net product generated by the land (not from precious metals as mercantilists believed)
  • Only agriculture produces "net wealth"; industry and trade are "sterile" — they transform but do not create new wealth
  • François Quesnay created the first comprehensive economic table in 1758 (precursor to national accounting)
  • Precursors of liberalism: "Let men act and let goods pass"
Turgot's experiment: Minister Turgot (1727-1781) applied liberal ideas: he liberalized cereal and flour trade in 1764, introduced freedom of movement and pricing for grain. However, following poor harvests, prices rose sharply, riots broke out, and he was forced to resign in 1776.

Physiocratic ideas remain relevant today in the context of competition between nations over water, fossil fuels, and rare earths. They help us understand the essential link between natural resources, political power, and social justice.

II. Standard Economics and Its Blinders

A. The Classical School and the Sanctification of Exchange Value

1. The Context of the Industrial Revolution: A Change in Values

Classical economists experienced the industrial revolution and observed:

  • Transition from craftsmanship to industry
  • Transformation of agriculture
  • Technical innovations (spinning wheel, steam engine)
  • Changing attitudes: everyone sought their own personal happiness

The objective of classical economics was to increase "the wealth of nations" and improve general well-being.

2. The Labour Theory of Value and the Obsession with Productivity

Use value: The satisfaction that a good provides (e.g., water in the desert).
Exchange value: The quantity of other goods that can be obtained in exchange (e.g., a diamond).
Classical economics only considers exchange value: the value of a good comes from the amount of labour required to produce it.
Adam Smith's pin factory: If one worker makes a pin alone, it takes much longer than dividing the work among several workers assigned to specific tasks. Specialization and division of labour increase productivity.

3. Supply-Side Economics, the Invisible Hand, and Trickle-Down

According to Jean-Baptiste Say, markets are self-regulating: supply creates its own demand (Say's Law of Markets), so there can be no crisis of overproduction.

The Invisible Hand (Adam Smith): Our selfish and individualistic choices lead, through a trickle-down effect, to wealth and the common good. "The sum of private interests leads to the general interest." The state's role is limited to sovereign functions (police, justice, defence) and enforcing property rights. This is the "police state."

4. Nature Has No Value

Classical blind spot: Nature has no value in itself. It acquires value only through the labour incorporated by humans. Resources from the earth are considered unlimited because they are free. As Jean-Baptiste Say wrote in 1803: "Natural resources are inexhaustible because otherwise we would not obtain them for free."

B. The Neoclassical School: Rationality, Utility, and the Market

With the neoclassical school, economics broke with philosophy, ethics, and justice. It became a mathematical science of optimal production when resources are scarce and needs are insatiable.

1. The Concept of Utility

Marginal utility: The additional satisfaction obtained by consuming one more unit of a good. Example: the first glass of water when thirsty = high marginal utility; the tenth glass = very low or negative. The value of a good becomes subjective (based on utility to the consumer, not labour).

2. Homo Economicus, Self-Interest, and Cost-Benefit Analysis

Each individual is a Homo economicus: selfish, rational, and a maximizer. Key assumptions about preferences:

PropertyMeaning
RationalConsumers compare goods and always choose what they prefer most
CompleteConsumers can always rank any two options (A preferred to B, B preferred to A, or indifferent)
TransitiveIf A > B and B > C, then A > C — no contradictory choices
Non-satiationMore is always better than less
Utility functionA mathematical function assigns a satisfaction score to each basket of goods
Cost-benefit analysis: When making decisions, individuals weigh costs (money, time, effort) against benefits (utility, profit, satisfaction) and choose the option with the greatest net benefit. Consumers maximize utility; firms maximize profit.

Neoclassical models generally only take financial constraints into account, neglecting ecological factors (pollution, habitat loss) that impose additional real-world limits.

Behavioural Economics: Challenging Homo Economicus

The Dictator Game: You are given £10 and can keep all or give some to a stranger. In theory, a purely selfish agent keeps everything. In reality, most people give £2-5 to strangers. This demonstrates that humans do not always act out of pure self-interest — they can be altruistic, supportive, or guided by values (justice, generosity).

The Global Preferences Survey across 70 countries confirmed that preferences vary greatly: patience is high in Scandinavia; risk tolerance is higher in the US and West Africa; altruism is more pronounced in European and Latin American countries. "Single" neoclassical rationality does not correspond to observed reality.

3. General Equilibrium, Pareto Optimum, and Pure and Perfect Competition (PPC)

General equilibrium: Supply = demand in all markets simultaneously; no one has an incentive to change behavior.
Pareto optimum: A situation where it is not possible to improve someone's position without worsening someone else's. This does not mean the distribution is fair — only that one cannot "do better" without harming someone.

The five conditions of pure and perfect competition:

  1. Atomicity: No agent can manipulate prices; many suppliers and consumers
  2. Free entry/exit: Companies can freely enter or leave the market
  3. Homogeneity: Price is the only factor influencing purchases
  4. Transparency: Free, instant, and complete information
  5. Mobility of production factors: Factors can move freely

C. Economic Reality: The Omnipresence of Market Failures

1. Pure and Perfect Competition: An Economic Myth

In reality, PPC assumptions are rarely verified:

  • Atomicity is not respected: there are power relations in markets
  • Entry/exit is not always free (regulatory barriers)
  • Homogeneity is not respected: firms differentiate their offerings
  • Information is often imperfect and asymmetric
  • Prices are not always flexible (downward rigidity, "sticky wages")
  • Herd behaviour: our choices are not independent (creates financial instability)

2. The Environment as an Externality

Externality: An effect (positive or negative) that an economic action has on people who are neither directly involved in that action nor compensated (or penalized) for that effect. It is a "collateral" consequence that affects third parties without passing through the market.
TypeExamples
Negative externalitiesPollution, resource depletion
Positive externalitiesInnovation, vaccination, education, biodiversity benefits

Externalities lead to a divergence between private costs/benefits and social costs/benefits, resulting in inefficient market outcomes. Government can intervene through regulations, taxes, subsidies (e.g., Pigou tax, 1920) to align private costs with social costs (internalization of costs).

Heterodox critique: Ecological economists argue that treating nature's services as mere externalities fails to recognize their intrinsic value. Externalities are not just a technical problem but symptomatic of deeper systemic problems. They persist because powerful actors benefit from externalizing costs. The economy must be reshaped through legislation, public investment, and community control — not just price changes.

3. The Environment as a Public Good

Public good: A good with two characteristics:
• Non-rivalry: Consumption by one person does not reduce the quantity available to others
• Non-excludability: Impossible (or very costly) to prevent someone from enjoying it
High ExcludabilityLow Excludability
High RivalryPrivate goods (clothing, food)Common resources (water, forests)
Low RivalryClub goods (Netflix, Wi-Fi)Public goods (street lighting, vaccination)
Free rider problem: Free riders consume more than their fair share or pay less than their fair share of the cost of the good. To finance public goods, the government uses taxation.

Solutions for managing public assets:

  • Hybrid solutions: Public-private partnerships (e.g., REDD+ initiative combining carbon credits with public monitoring and community involvement)
  • Collective management: Elinor Ostrom (1990) showed that local communities can manage common goods sustainably with clear rules, enforcement mechanisms, and trust in self-governance structures

D. The Keynesian School and Its Biases

1. Short-Term Thinking

Keynes (1883-1946) observed the crisis of the 1930s and analyzed the failure of "laissez-faire." Markets left to their own devices do not necessarily lead to economic optimum; imbalances between supply and demand can be long-lasting.

The short-term bias: Keynes advocated short-term analysis: "In the long run, we are all dead." From that point, the short term became the economic compass: prosperity was pitted against posterity. Financial capitalism became "capitalism without a plan" — failing to create wealth and improve long-term well-being.

2. Demand as the Source of Growth

According to Keynes, it is effective demand that determines production, investment, and employment. Growth became a top priority in the 1930s with GDP measurements. However, the more we consume and produce, the more we strain the planet:

  • Depleting resources
  • Generating pollution and waste
  • Disrupting the climate

Conclusion

The economy, originally a means of organizing the household, has become a system that organizes everything. Economic discipline has detached itself from its initial ethical project and has been reduced to a mathematical, formalized order.

Key question: Does the economy need to undergo radical reform or simply return to the origins of economic thinking dating back thousands of years? As Albert Einstein said: "We cannot solve problems with the same thinking we used when we created them."
