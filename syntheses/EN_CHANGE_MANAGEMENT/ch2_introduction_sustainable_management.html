Summary — Change Management Ch 2: Introduction to Sustainable Management

Part I: From Rhetoric to Measurement

Why Measure What Really Matters

Organizations now face binding carbon budgets, stricter disclosure rules, and growing investor pressure. Yet most management tools (GDP growth, ESG ratings, profit margins) were never designed for a resource-constrained world. Environmental performance should be managed with the same precision as financial performance.

The gap: Traditional ESG scores mix intentions and outcomes, creating confusion. Many firms communicate more than they transform. Sustainability requires verifiable data on what companies actually consume and emit.

From Rhetoric to Measurement: Why Firms Need Reliable Indicators

Without reliable data, sustainability stays at the level of promises or public relations. Firms need clear, comparable, and auditable indicators, tracking:

  • How much carbon is emitted per unit of value created
  • How much energy (including renewables) and water the company uses
  • How much waste is generated, reused, or recycled

The Four Dimensions of Environmental Performance

DimensionFocusKey Goal
1. CarbonGHG emissions from operations, logistics, energyDecarbonize production and supply chains
2. EnergyEfficiency and share of renewable energyProduce more value with less energy
3. WaterQuantity used and treated; consumption vs. recyclingPreserve resources, minimize water stress

Together, these indicators help firms assess their true environmental performance, compare with peers, and guide strategic investments. Valayden (PRISM–Sorbonne) developed a composite environmental performance index combining these four dimensions into one measurable score.

The Decoupling Challenge

Many firms celebrate relative progress ("we grew faster than emissions"). However, the real task is absolute decoupling — reducing total emissions while still growing economically.

Kaya Identity:
CO2 = Population × (GDP / Population) × (Energy / GDP) × (CO2 / Energy)

Managers control only the last two ratios: energy intensity and carbon intensity. Sustainability means producing more value with less energy and fewer emissions.

What Drives Real Improvement

  1. Cleaner Production: Redesign processes to prevent pollution at the source rather than treating it afterward
  2. Learning Curves: Technology costs fall as production scales — early adopters benefit most
  3. Governance & Incentives: Managers act on what boards reward; link pay to auditable environmental metrics

Case Study: Ørsted's Transformation

From Fossil Dependence to Renewable Leadership:
  • Background (2006): Ørsted (then DONG Energy) was 85% dependent on coal and gas, producing nearly one-third of Denmark's CO2 emissions
  • Crisis as Catalyst: The 2014 oil price collapse exposed fossil assets as risky and unprofitable
  • Strategic Shift: Sold/converted fossil plants, redirected investment into offshore wind
  • Results: By 2019, 86% of electricity from renewables; profits increased as emissions fell; ranked #1 in Global 100 Sustainability Index (2020)
Key Lesson: Sustainability is smart strategy when markets, technology, and regulation all move toward low-carbon systems.

Management Lessons from Ørsted

  • Leadership Commitment: CEO and board framed decarbonization as business transformation, not CSR
  • Strategic Reallocation: Divested fossil assets, reinvested in offshore wind
  • Governance & Measurement: Clear environmental targets tied to executive incentives
  • Cultural Transformation: Employees retrained for renewables
  • System Perspective: Collaboration with suppliers, regulators, and investors

Understanding Stranded Assets

Stranded asset: An investment that loses its value earlier than expected because of environmental, market, or policy change. Oil fields, coal plants, or pipelines can become unprofitable when cleaner technologies become cheaper, regulations rise, or investors demand greener portfolios.
NPV formula: Assets lose value when expected cash flows fall or risk (discount rate) rises. If profits fall or risk increases, the asset's value collapses — it becomes "stranded."

Managerial implication: Ignoring environmental risk can destroy shareholder value. Sustainable change is both a risk management strategy and a moral choice.

What Does "Sustainability" Really Mean?

Triple Bottom Line — People, Planet, Profit:
  • Environmental Dimension: Protecting natural systems — reducing waste, emissions, preserving biodiversity
  • Social Dimension: Promoting fairness, inclusion, human well-being — safe work, equity, community development
  • Economic Dimension: Ensuring financial viability and innovation while internalizing environmental and social costs
These are not separate pillars but interdependent gears — harming one ultimately weakens the others.

The Brundtland Definition (1987)

"Development that meets the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs." — World Commission on Environment and Development, 1987.

Key principles: intergenerational equity (fairness across time), holistic balance (economic, environmental, social progress together), and sustainability as a moral and strategic commitment.

Managerial meaning: Business decisions must adopt a longer time horizon, balancing quarterly returns with generational resilience.

Part II: Diagnosing Unsustainability and Systemic Change

Is Our Current Economic Model Sustainable?

Reality check: Global growth has been achieved through depletion of natural capital faster than it can regenerate. Fossil fuel use has risen eightfold since 1950. Energy-related CO2 emissions surpassed 37 billion tonnes in 2023 (record high). Efficiency gains have not compensated for the sheer scale of consumption.

Structural Drivers of Unsustainability

  1. Population & Consumption Growth: From 1 billion (1800) to 8+ billion today; material consumption per person multiplied several times
  2. Land Use Pressure: ~50% of habitable land converted for agriculture; consequences include deforestation, biodiversity loss, soil degradation
  3. Fossil-Fuel Dependence: Global energy remains ~80% fossil-based; CO2 levels exceed 420 ppm

Planetary Boundaries

Planetary Boundaries Framework (Rockström et al., 2009; Steffen et al., 2015): Defines nine ecological limits maintaining Earth-system stability (climate change, biodiversity loss, freshwater use, nitrogen/phosphorus cycles, chemical pollution). Several boundaries — especially climate, biosphere integrity, and nutrient cycles — have already been transgressed.

Strong Sustainability Principle: Natural capital is non-substitutable; once critical ecological functions degrade, economic capital cannot replace them.

Sustainability Hype vs. Reality

The Performance-Perception Gap: Sustainability has become a marketing narrative. Fossil fuels still provide 80% of global energy, and emissions keep rising. Many firms practice greenwashing — using sustainability branding to gain legitimacy without changing core operations. The outcome is a credibility crisis: abundant discourse, little decarbonization.

From Individual Action to Institutional Transformation

Recycling, conscious consumption, and energy saving are important but limited. The decisive levers lie in institutional systems — corporations, governments, financial markets, and global supply chains. Individual morality cannot compensate for flawed system design.

Sustainability gap: Economic and political systems are dominated by short-term horizons (firms optimize quarterly results, governments act within election cycles). Sustainability benefits accrue long-term, creating a gap between private and social time horizons.

Porter Hypothesis: Well-designed regulation can enhance competitiveness by stimulating innovation.

Three Layers of Institutional Change

  1. Rules: Legislation, standards, and taxation reshape cost structures
  2. Norms: Public opinion and cultural expectations redefine legitimacy
  3. Beliefs: Cognitive frames within organizations determine what is "normal" or "successful"

Policy without belief change yields compliance, not conviction.

From Linear to Circular Systems

The Linear Economy: "Take → Make → Use → Dispose"

The 20th-century industrial system is linear: materials flow in one direction from extraction to waste. This assumes infinite inputs and sinks — a false premise under ecological constraint.

The Circular Economy Paradigm

Circular economy: Transforms linear flows into loops through Reuse (extend product life), Remanufacture/Recycling (recover materials and energy), and Regeneration (rebuild natural capital). The central ratio: Resource Productivity = Value Added / Material Input. Circularity is a new industrial metabolism decoupling value creation from resource depletion.

Planetary Economics: Rethinking Growth

  • Tim Jackson (2009): "Prosperity Without Growth" — well-being stems from social trust and meaning, not consumption volume
  • Kate Raworth (2017): "Doughnut Economics" — safe and just space between social foundation and ecological ceiling
  • Herman Daly: Steady-state economy — quantitative limits, qualitative improvement
Case Study: Walmart's Project Gigaton: Launched 2017, goal to avoid 1 gigaton CO2 by 2030 across suppliers (equal to Japan's yearly emissions). Tackled Scope 3 emissions across six areas: energy, waste, packaging, transport, nature, product design. By 2024, early success announced. Lesson: Large firms can act as system orchestrators.

From Awareness to Execution

Despite widespread commitment, most firms fail to operationalize sustainability due to internal barriers:

  • Institutional inertia (legacy systems)
  • Short-term metrics (quarterly focus)
  • Fragmented accountability (no central ownership)
  • Skill gaps (limited technical expertise)
  • Cultural skepticism (seen as "non-core")

Measurement as the Engine of Change

"What gets measured gets managed — and what gets compared gets improved." Embedding verified indicators (carbon intensity, water use, waste, energy efficiency) into dashboards and incentives maintains momentum and credibility. Data transforms sustainability from an image exercise to a management discipline.

Learning from Failure and Theories of Change

BP Deepwater Horizon (2010): Explosion killed 11 workers; 4+ million barrels of oil spilled. Cost: over $60 billion. Failure stemmed from cost-cutting culture, fragmented responsibilities, weak oversight. Sustainability and safety were treated as compliance, not strategic risks. Lesson: Sustainability failures are governance failures.

Organizational Myopia (Dorothy Leonard, 2001)

Strengths can harden into weaknesses. Firms fail to adapt due to cognitive lock-in, sunk-cost bias, and incentive misalignment. Solution: strategic unlearning — redefining efficiency, growth, and value creation under ecological constraint.

Systemic Complementarities: The Architecture of Inertia

  • Technological lock-in: Fossil infrastructure
  • Institutional lock-in: Subsidies and laws favouring incumbents
  • Professional lock-in: Accounting ignores natural capital
  • Cultural lock-in: Consumer expectations of abundance

From CSR to Shared Value

Shared Value (Porter & Kramer, 2011): Firms can create economic advantage by addressing social and environmental problems through innovation and system redesign — not philanthropy. Three mechanisms:
  1. Reconceiving products & markets: Design offerings meeting unmet social needs
  2. Redefining value-chain productivity: Eliminate waste, energy loss, resource inefficiency
  3. Enabling local cluster development: Invest in supplier capabilities and community infrastructure
Unlike CSR, shared value is embedded in corporate strategy, linking purpose to profit through innovation.

Managerial Readiness for Sustainability Transformation

Four levers of readiness:

  1. Leadership commitment: Clear tone from the top; board accountability
  2. Cross-functional integration: Collaboration across finance, operations, HR, supply chain
  3. Incentive alignment: Executive pay tied to environmental KPIs
  4. Learning & innovation culture: Ability to unlearn old routines and experiment
Lewin's Change Model (1947):
  1. Unfreeze: Question norms and incentives
  2. Move: Experiment and institutionalize new practices
  3. Refreeze (flexible): Embed new routines yet keep adaptive capacity alive

Concluding Synthesis

  • Failures (BP) = governance blind spots; environment treated as compliance
  • Successes (Ørsted, Walmart) = systemic integration of sustainability into strategy
  • Theoretical lenses: institutional adaptation, behavioral learning, systems transformation
  • Managerial imperative: Align value creation with ecological viability and measure rigorously
Bottom line: Sustainability is both ethical imperative and strategic evolution. Firms applying an empirical approach demonstrate that rigor in measurement drives real transformation.
