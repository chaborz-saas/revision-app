Summary — Change Management Ch 3: From Rhetoric to Strategy

Part I: Measuring and Prioritizing Sustainability

Why Focus Matters: Selecting Issues That Count

Modern firms face dozens of ESG challenges, but effective strategy requires focus and materiality. Porter & Kramer (2006) classify sustainability issues into three types:

Issue TypeDefinitionExample
1. Generic IssuesSocially important but unrelated to the firm's operationsA software firm donating to disaster relief
2. Value-Chain ImpactsDirect outcomes of the firm's activitiesLabour conditions, emissions, logistics
3. Competitive Context IssuesStructural conditions shaping long-term competitivenessInfrastructure, skills, environmental regulation
Strategic sustainability lies where business advantage and societal progress converge. Generic issues build goodwill but offer limited leverage. Value-chain issues are directly controllable. Competitive-context issues shape the "rules of the game" and offer the highest systemic leverage.

Criteria for Strategic Prioritization: Materiality Analysis

Managers evaluate sustainability issues through three analytical filters:

  1. Influence: Where can the firm truly shape outcomes (policy, innovation, procurement)?
  2. Risk Exposure: Where lie the biggest regulatory, reputational, or operational threats?
  3. Impact Potential: Which actions yield both societal and business value?
Double Materiality: Combines financial materiality (impact of sustainability issues on the firm's finances) with environmental materiality (impact of the firm on the environment). Tools include materiality mapping (GRI, SASB). Strategic resources are allocated where leverage and impact overlap.

From Mapping to Measurement: Operationalizing Materiality

Mapping tells you what matters; metrics tell you how much and where to act. Valayden's composite index (PRISM–Sorbonne) provides comparable scores for carbon, energy, water, and waste. This allows managers to identify "hot spots," rank ROI, track progress, and communicate credibly.

Why Firms Engage in Sustainability: Four Rationales

RationaleDescriptionLimitation
(a) Moral ImperativeEthical duty, intergenerational justiceDefines intention, not priorities or trade-offs
(b) Stewardship LogicTriple Bottom Line — People, Planet, ProfitHolistic but vague without metrics or governance
(c) Attention-Based LogicReact to stakeholder visibility & investor pressureReactive to media cycles → short-term responses
(d) License & ReputationMaintain legitimacy, avoid sanctionsRisk management → possible greenwashing
Common flaw: These rationales answer why to act, not how to create value. The result is fragmented CSR initiatives with weak authority and unclear impact.

Bridges to Strategic Sustainability

To move from justification to integration, two complementary lenses are needed:

  1. Institutional Change Models: Explain how norms, rules, and governance mechanisms evolve (e.g., carbon taxes, mandatory disclosure, board-level oversight)
  2. Circular Business Innovation Frameworks: Show how firms redesign products, supply chains, and revenue models to decouple growth from resource use

From CSR to CSM (Corporate Sustainability Management)

Evolution in three steps:
  1. CSR: Voluntary and narrative
  2. ESG: Investor compliance and reporting
  3. CSM: Data-driven strategic management — measure impacts through indices and life-cycle tools, manage through incentives and governance, improve through continuous innovation and feedback loops

From Moral Awareness to Managerial Integration

Three cumulative stages:

  1. Moral awareness: Recognize ethical and ecological imperatives
  2. Measurement: Translate principles into quantified indicators
  3. Managerial integration: Embed targets into budgets, KPIs, and decision processes

Part II: Institutional & Business-Model Innovation for Sustainability

From "Why" to "How": Embedding Sustainability in Strategy

The question is no longer why firms act, but how they execute. The shift is from "sustainability as discourse" to "sustainability as competitive logic." Strategy is redefined: beyond cost leadership or differentiation toward value alignment — creating advantage by solving social and environmental problems.

The Social Logic of Strategy: Shared Value

Shared Value (Porter & Kramer, 2011): Business and society are interdependent. Firms need healthy ecosystems, skilled labour, stable communities. Society needs productive firms for jobs, taxes, innovation. The question is not "how to limit harm" but how to expand mutual benefit.

Three Mechanisms of Shared Value

MechanismDescriptionGain
1. Reconceive products & marketsInnovate solutions for unmet needs (e.g., microgrids for remote communities)New markets + impact
2. Redefine value-chain productivityLower energy, water, waste; safer work; better sourcingEfficiency + resilience
3. Enable local cluster developmentTrain suppliers, improve infrastructure, support smallholdersQuality, reliability, lower systemic risk

Virtuous circle: Solving societal problems → innovation → lower costs → reputation & license to operate.

From Compliance to Competitive Logic

In practice, strategic sustainability means:

  • Put environmental and social metrics on executive dashboards
  • Pay for performance on audited ESG outcomes
  • Use sustainability as an innovation filter (screen R&D, capex)
  • Treat climate, inequality, biodiversity as core strategy, not PR

Why Shared Value = Competitive Advantage

It is not philanthropy; it is innovation under constraint.
  • Economic: Efficiency gains, cost reduction, new markets
  • Social: Health, education, environmental quality
  • Reputational: Trust with customers, employees, investors
Three waves: 1) Profit maximization → 2) CSR risk control → 3) Shared value creation.
Mindset: Constraint = design brief for advantage.

Strategic Prioritization & Value Chain Transformation

Problem: dispersion — trying to fix everything leads to diluted results. Focus where there is:

  • Influence: We can shape the outcome
  • Exposure: We must act
  • Differentiation: We can win
Same Problem, Different Stakes (Climate Example):
  • Best Buy (retail): Generic issue → focus on store efficiency, brand
  • Delta (aviation): Value-chain issue → fleet, fuels, operations
  • ExxonMobil (energy): Competitive-context issue → the core business model at risk
Lesson: Firm-specific strategy. Use comparable metrics to find leverage.

Addressing Value-Chain Externalities

Beyond reporting (GRI), move to analytics and action with:

  • LCA (Life Cycle Analysis): Quantify impacts across the lifecycle
  • Supply-chain mapping: Identify high-risk inputs, routes, geographies
  • Scope 3 accounting: Upstream + downstream emissions

Regulatory tailwind: EU CSRD makes disclosure mandatory — early movers shape standards and avoid retrofits.

Inside-Out vs. Outside-In

Inside-Out: Reduce harms from our operations (waste, emissions, labour).
Outside-In: Address external conditions that affect competitiveness (infrastructure, health, water).
Strategic integration asks: (1) Which societal issues are most material? (2) How can solving them enhance performance? This is the foundation for shared value.
Case: HUL Project Shakti (Inclusive Sustainability): Trained rural women in India as micro-entrepreneurs selling HUL products. Scale: 120,000+ women across 18 states by 2023. Social impact: income, empowerment, community resilience. Strategic gain: rural reach, trusted local distribution. Lesson: Inclusion can be a supply-chain innovation, not just CSR.
Case: Facebook & Cambridge Analytica: Data misuse; weak governance of social externalities (privacy, misinformation). Consequences: regulatory backlash, trust loss, high reputational and financial cost. Missed opportunity: lead on privacy and algorithmic transparency. Takeaway: Sustainability includes digital responsibility — data rights, equity, truth.

Key Takeaways

  1. Materiality is contextual — tailor by sector, geography, business model
  2. Measurement enables focus — avoid rhetoric; fund leverage points
  3. Early action creates advantage — anticipate norms and regulation
  4. Externalities = markets — problems become product/process innovations
