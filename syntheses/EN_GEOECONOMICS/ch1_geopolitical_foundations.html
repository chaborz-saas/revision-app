Summary — Geoeconomics Ch 1: Geopolitical Foundations and Geoeconomics

1. What is Geopolitics?

Geopolitics = the study of rivalries for power and/or influence over a given territory (Lacoste, 2008). The central question is: What is at stake in a given territory that makes it a place of crisis, conflict, or tension?

Geopolitics examines the world as a chessboard divided into territories that are the focus and theatre of confrontations involving various actors. Rivalries can take multiple forms: military wars, economic competition, electoral competition, diplomatic disputes, information warfare, and more.

1.1 Three Key Dimensions of Geopolitical Analysis

Any geopolitical analysis revolves around three interconnected dimensions:

  • Territory — the space over which rivalries take place
  • Actors — the entities involved in the confrontation
  • Issue / Stake / Conflict — what is being fought over

1.2 Types of Actors

Geopolitical actors are diverse and operate at local, national, and international scales:

  • States — the traditional sovereign actors
  • Intra-state actors — political parties, separatist movements, trade unions
  • Trans-state actors — multinational corporations, international institutions
  • Non-state actors — NGOs, lobbies, criminal organizations, citizens/consumers, financial actors

1.3 Internal vs. External Actors

Internal actors: actors directly in opposition through crises/conflicts/tensions and directly involved in an issue in a given territory.

External actors: actors who have friendly or hostile relations with internal actors and/or who have other interests in the territory where internal actors are in conflict, and who may therefore have an impact on the issue.

For each actor, three analytical questions apply:

  1. How do they view the issue and its components?
  2. What are their respective interests?
  3. What are they capable of doing to defend their respective interests?

Issue: Territorial sovereignty over Jammu and Kashmir.
Internal actors: Indian Union government vs. Kashmiri separatist groups.
External actors: Pakistan (diplomatic opposition), China (military presence in Ladakh), United Nations (human rights critiques), USA (encourages dialogue).

3. Power: Hard Power and Soft Power

Power = "The capacity of an actor in the system to act on other actors and on the system itself to defend what it believes to be its interests, achieve its objectives, and preserve or even strengthen its supremacy" (Verluise, 2013).
Two types of power levers:
Hard power: the ability to influence another actor through coercive force (military, economic, technological).
Soft power: the ability to influence another actor through non-coercive means (cultural, ideological).

Both types can be used in varying proportions by different actors involved in an issue. States are not the only actors endowed with power.

4. Economic Power: Geoeconomics

Geoeconomics = the use of economic instruments by States for strategic purposes within the international arena (Luttwak, 1990). It is grounded in the notion that the economy serves as a tool of power rather than merely a domain of cooperation (Lorot, 2025). It bridges market logic with political objectives (Babic, Dixon & Liu, 2022).

4.1 Key Characteristics of Geoeconomics

  • Employed both in times of war and peace, reflecting underlying great-power rivalry (Diesen, 2021)
  • Closely tied to economic sovereignty, particularly in the context of interdependence
  • Challenges the notion of a self-regulating global market by emphasizing the active role of states (Shahzad, 2019)
  • Highlights the resurgence of national economic policies within globalization (Babic et al., 2022)
  • Reveals the blurring boundaries between the economic and geopolitical spheres (Diesen, 2021)

4.2 Geoeconomics vs. Geopolitics

DimensionGeopoliticsGeoeconomics
FocusPower rivalries grounded in territorial controlRivalries rooted in economic flows
DomainTerritorial sovereigntyGlobal economic competition
InstrumentsMilitary dimensions, explicit use of forceEconomic instruments, indirect forms of constraint
LogicPower through space and territoryCosts, incentives, and dependencies
ApproachDirect confrontationStrategic exploitation of asymmetric interdependencies
Key insight: The distinction between geopolitics and geoeconomics is increasingly blurred, as hybrid strategies proliferate (Shahzad, 2022; Diesen, 2021). They are complementary approaches: distinct but interrelated modalities of power. Contemporary State strategies increasingly combine both logics.

4.3 Geoeconomic Levers of Power

Geoeconomics involves the strategic deployment of economic tools to exert influence, lying at the intersection of international economics and State strategy:

CategoryGeoeconomic Levers
Trade instrumentsEconomic sanctions and embargoes, tariff manipulation, politically conditioned or asymmetric trade agreements
Resource controlConditional access to critical resources (e.g., rare earth elements), instrumentalization of energy dependencies
TechnologyExport controls on sensitive technologies, digital networks and data control
Industrial policyStrategic industrial policies (subsidies to key sectors), foreign direct investment
InfrastructureUse of transport infrastructure, infrastructure control
FinanceMonetary instruments, strategic investments, credit diplomacy
RegulationNorms and regulations, energy and raw material diplomacy

5. Contemporary Issues in Geoeconomics

  • The rise in geopolitical tensions is reviving the use of geoeconomic tools (Mohr & Trebesch, 2025)
  • The control of key technologies (AI, semiconductors) is becoming a central strategic lever
  • Asymmetric interdependencies create vulnerabilities that can be exploited geoeconomically
  • Geoeconomics strengthens the need for coherent national industrial strategies
  • The US-China decoupling is reshaping global economic governance
Bottom line: Geoeconomics highlights the centrality of controlling resources, flows, and standards. It prioritizes costs, incentives, and dependencies over military confrontation, and favors indirect action through economic instruments.
