Summary — Geoeconomics Ch 2: Actors, Flows, and Scales of Analysis

1. Case Studies: Analyzing Geoeconomic Crises

1.1 TikTok vs. USA

In 2024, the US Congress passed the Protecting Americans from Foreign Adversary Controlled Applications Act (PAFACA), targeting TikTok's parent company ByteDance. The law required divestment or a nationwide ban. In 2025, the Supreme Court upheld the law, prioritizing national security over corporate and user rights.

Internal actors:
• US Government (Congress, Biden administration): safeguard national security, limit foreign access to American data, assert digital sovereignty.
• ByteDance/TikTok: retain access to the US market (most profitable), preserve data-driven business model, defend reputation as a "global" (not "Chinese") company.

External actors:
• China: protect technology champions, prevent precedent of forced divestment, maintain indirect access to Western data flows.
• US users and creators: keep a central platform for entertainment, communication, and income generation.

Flows at stake:

  • Data flows: continuous transfer and processing of US user data
  • Information flows: TikTok's algorithm influences public discourse and cultural trends (disinformation concerns)
  • Financial flows: advertising revenues generated in the US market

Potential conflicts: US Gov. vs. ByteDance (data sovereignty); ByteDance vs. China (compliance vs. market survival); US Gov. vs. users (national security vs. free expression).

1.2 Foxconn/Apple vs. China

In 2023, Chinese authorities launched tax and land-use inspections targeting Foxconn subsidiaries (the world's largest electronics manufacturer and Apple's primary iPhone assembler), amid intensifying US-China technological rivalry over semiconductors.

Internal actors:
• Chinese Government: preserve control over strategic value chains, use regulatory measures (inspections, fiscal audits) as geoeconomic tools in the US-China rivalry.
• Foxconn (and Apple): secure iPhone production, ensure continuity of component/product flows, minimize regulatory risks.

External actors:
• United States: reduce Apple's dependence on Chinese manufacturing, promote relocation to India/Vietnam.
• Alternative production hubs (India, Vietnam): attract assembly flows, secure investments and jobs.

Flows at stake:

  • Material flows: components (semiconductors, batteries, screens) and finished products (iPhones)
  • Financial flows: taxation, profits, potential penalties from regulatory actions
  • Knowledge flows: transfer of know-how and manufacturing expertise

1.3 Lukoil vs. Ukraine

In June 2024, Ukraine tightened sanctions on Russian companies and restricted Lukoil's oil transit through the Druzhba pipeline to Central Europe. Hungary and Slovakia, heavily dependent on Russian crude, protested. A commercial transit issue escalated into a diplomatic dispute, illustrating the strategic use of energy flows in wartime.

Internal actors:
• Ukraine: enforce sanctions, weaken Russian influence, use control over transit flows as leverage.
• Lukoil: ensure delivery to Central Europe, fulfill contractual obligations, maintain revenue.

External actors:
• Hungary and Slovakia: guarantee energy security, avoid shortages and price spikes.
• European Union: preserve sanctions cohesion, manage divergent member-state interests, reduce dependence on Russian energy.

Flows at stake: Energy flows (crude oil through pipelines), financial flows (oil sales revenues, transit fees), secondary resource flows (refined products in Central Europe).

1.4 Tesla Gigafactory Berlin-Brandenburg

In 2024, Tesla faced sustained opposition to the expansion of its Gigafactory in Grunheide, Brandenburg. Environmental activist groups protested, attempted site intrusions, and claimed responsibility for sabotage disrupting electricity supply. The conflict exposed tensions between industrial ambitions and environmental concerns.

Internal actors: Tesla (expand EV production, secure resources) vs. Environmental activist groups (prevent deforestation and water consumption).
External actors: German federal government (showcase industrial transition), European consumers/investors (reliable EV supply).
Flows: Resource flows (water, electricity), material flows (components imported, vehicles exported), financial flows (investments, potential losses from disruptions).
Territories: Local (Grunheide) and broader European markets.

1.5 Airbnb vs. New York City

In 2023, NYC implemented Local Law 18, introducing strict registration requirements for short-term rentals, significantly reducing Airbnb listings. This created a test case for regulating the platform economy at the local level.

Internal actors: NYC authorities (control rental market, protect housing) vs. Airbnb and local hosts (maintain rental activity and revenues).
External actors: Hotel industry and neighborhood associations (reduce competition), tourists (access affordable accommodation).
Flows: Financial (rental revenues, taxes), tourism (visitor inflows), data (registration/compliance data).
Territories: NYC boroughs (local), other US cities and global tourism flows (broader).

2. The Different Actors in the Geoeconomic Arena

2.1 States: Actors with Multiple Logics

  • States may pursue geopolitical, geoeconomic, or hybrid strategies depending on context (Agnew, 2003)
  • Their power relies on control of borders, resources, and representations (Tuathail, 1999)
  • State strategies evolve according to scale: local, national, regional, global (Moisio, 2015)
  • The Nation-State remains dominant but its authority is increasingly contested (Agnew & Corbridge, 2003)

2.2 Multinational Corporations (MNCs): Major Geoeconomic Actors

MNCs influence geopolitics through their choices of location, sourcing, and lobbying (Flint, 2021). They can either reinforce or bypass State sovereignty (Devilla, 2023). Their power derives from control over global value chains (Bednarski et al., 2023). Some MNCs engage in influence strategies or are instrumentalized by States (Belhoste & Dimitrova, 2023).

2.3 Legal Non-State Actors

  • NGOs influence the international agenda on human rights and environment (Kuus, 2017)
  • International institutions define norms and regulations that structure power relations (Moisio, 2015)
  • Think tanks and media shape geopolitical representations (Dodds, 2007)

2.4 Illegal Non-State Actors

  • Organized criminal groups operate on transnational scales (Dussouy, 2010)
  • Terrorism mobilizes both symbolic and physical territories (Flint, 2021)
  • Some illegal actors can durably destabilize weak states (Tuathail et al., 1998)
Summary of actors: States remain central, but MNCs influence power through investments and value chains. NGOs, international institutions, and religious actors contribute to the reconfiguration of territories. The rise of non-state actors calls for a rethinking of sovereignty (Kuus, 2017).

3. Scales of Geopolitical and Geoeconomic Analysis

3.1 Revisiting Territoriality

  • Territoriality is no longer limited to State borders (Agnew, 2003)
  • Networks and flows (data, capital, migrants) are reshaping territories (Tuathail & Agnew, 1998)
  • Territorialization of firms is fluid, bypassing national constraints (Agnew & Corbridge, 2003)

3.2 Multiple Scales

Conflicts can be approached at different scales: local, national, regional, and global (Moisio, 2015). The scale shapes how issues are perceived and how actors are defined (Dussouy, 2010). Some local conflicts are instrumentalized for global geoeconomic purposes (Agnew, 2003).

3.3 Three Levels of Analysis

LevelFocusKey Characteristics
Classical GeopoliticsStruggles between States for territorial controlRooted in 19th-century strategic geography. Key figures: Ratzel (Raum), Mackinder (Heartland), Spykman (Rimland). State-centric, focuses on borders, resources, armed conflicts.
Critical GeopoliticsStruggles involving States and/or non-State actors over identity issuesFocuses on production of geopolitical discourses. Questions narratives and representations about space. Rejects geographical neutrality. Examines how knowledge contributes to power.
GeoeconomicsCompetition over flows, networks, and resourcesGlobalization redefines stakes. Conflicts shift towards flows. States adapt to transnational networks. Power measured by ability to influence global norms.

3.4 Limits of Classical Geopolitics

  • State-centric and deterministic view of conflicts (Kuus, 2017)
  • Underestimates the role of representations, ideologies, and non-state actors (Tuathail, 1999)
  • Poorly suited to contemporary hybrid conflicts (Belhoste & Dimitrova, 2023)

3.5 Critical Geopolitics: Representations and Discourses

  • Elite discourses shape perceptions of threats and enemies (Tuathail, 1999)
  • Cultural representations contribute to the construction of otherness and territory
  • Media play a role in constructing geopolitical narratives (Dodds, 2007)
  • Geopolitical imagination is fueled by films, TV series, video games, and literature
  • Maps and visualizations convey ideological messages (Agnew & Corbridge, 2003)

4. Geographies of Power: Territories, Networks, and Flows

  • Power is not limited to military force; it includes economic, normative, and technological influence (Flint, 2021)
  • Territory remains essential but in renewed forms (Babic et al., 2022)
  • Power projection relies on networks (cables, roads, logistical platforms) (Devilla, 2023)
  • Cyberspace and flows have become key dimensions of power (Flint, 2021)
Contested territories and identity: Conflicts over territorial control often involve strong identity dimensions. Historical and symbolic representations fuel disputes. Memory-based conflicts are used to legitimize geopolitical claims (Belhoste & Dimitrova, 2023).

5. Geoeconomics: Supply Chains and Integrated Reading

5.1 The Geopolitics of Supply Chains

  • Supply chains have become central geoeconomic issues
  • States are seeking to relocate or secure critical supply chains (Devilla, 2023)
  • Supply disruptions can be used as geoeconomic tools (Belhoste & Dimitrova, 2023)

5.2 Towards an Integrated Geopolitical-Geoeconomic Reading

Key synthesis: Geopolitical and geoeconomic dynamics are increasingly intertwined (De Villa, 2023). Understanding contemporary territories requires a multi-scale and multi-actor approach (Flint, 2021). Critical analysis helps move beyond simplistic interpretations of conflicts (Kuus, 2017; Belhoste & Dimitrova, 2023).

Globalization is redefining geopolitical stakes: conflicts are shifting towards flows (Agnew, 2003) and become geoeconomic stakes. States are adapting their strategies to transnational networks and interdependencies. Power is also measured by the ability to influence global norms (Kuus, 2017).
