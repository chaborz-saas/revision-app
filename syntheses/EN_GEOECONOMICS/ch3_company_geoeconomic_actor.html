Summary — Geoeconomics Ch 3: The Company as a Geoeconomic Actor

1. The Company as a Geoeconomic Actor

Transnational corporations (TNCs) are no longer mere economic agents; they influence geopolitics (Kobrin, 2015). Their strategic decisions have repercussions on power relations among States (Belhoste & Dimitrova, 2023). They operate in high-risk areas where States are not always predominant (De Villa et al., 2015).

Companies have become central players in the geoeconomic arena. Their choices regarding where to produce, where to source, and how to lobby all have geopolitical consequences. They are both subjects and objects of geoeconomic strategy: they act upon the international system while also being acted upon by States and other actors.

1.1 The Company in the Face of Geopolitical Multipolarity

  • The multipolar world exposes firms to complex strategic trade-offs (Kobrin, 2017)
  • Norm and sanction conflicts reshape the configuration of value chains (Tamimi, 2017)
  • Political instability influences location and investment choices (De Villa et al., 2023)
  • Firms must manage transnational and fluctuating geopolitical risks (Caldarai & Iacovello, 2022)
Key challenge: In a multipolar world, companies face competing norms, sanctions regimes, and political pressures from different states. A single investment or sourcing decision can place a company in the crossfire of rival geopolitical blocs.

1.2 The Company as a Normative Actor

  • Some multinationals influence the rules of the international game (Behrendt & Khanna, 2021)
  • They take part in the construction of standards, particularly in ecological or digital matters (Kobrin, 2015)
  • Their normative power depends on their size, sector, and lobbying capacity (Tamimi, 2017)
Example: Tech giants like Apple or Google shape digital standards globally. Energy companies like TotalEnergies influence climate norms through their lobbying. Pharmaceutical companies affect health standards through their patent strategies and regulatory influence.

2. Global Value Chains and Asymmetric Interdependencies

Global value chains: Companies structure interconnected global production networks (Kobrin, 2017). These networks generate interdependencies but also vulnerabilities (Babic, Dixon & Liu, 2022).
  • Companies must consider the resilience of their value chains (Caldarai & Iacovello, 2022)
  • Geopolitical risks are both highly unpredictable and intense (De Villa et al., 2015)
  • Asymmetric interdependencies mean that some actors in a chain are more vulnerable to disruption than others
  • A disruption at one node (e.g., semiconductor production in Taiwan) can cascade through the entire global economy
Strategic implication: The more interconnected a company's value chain, the more exposed it is to geopolitical shocks. Concentration of production in a single country creates a single point of failure that rivals can exploit.

4. Geoeconomic Diagnostic of a Company

A structured geoeconomic diagnostic follows six steps:

Step 1: Characterize the Company

  • Mission, vision, values, and history
  • Production: production volume, production territories, sales territories, sales flows
  • Factors of production: supply territories, supply flows
  • Areas of activity: demographic, economic, legal characteristics of territories

Step 2: Analyze Stakeholders

  • Their mission, vision, values, and history (including future expansion plans)
  • Company's level of dependence on the stakeholder: percentage of supply/sales relying on the stakeholder, strategic importance (reputation, network)
  • Stakeholder's level of dependence on the company: percentage of stakeholder's activity represented by the company
  • Stakeholder's stability: for a company, market position and financial situation; for an institutional actor, membership and reputation; for a political actor, government stability and electoral sensitivity
Interdependence analysis: Understanding the mutual dependency between a company and its stakeholders is central to assessing geoeconomic vulnerability. High dependence on a single stakeholder in a geopolitically unstable region is a critical risk factor.

Step 3: Analyze Areas of Activity

  • Identify crises/conflicts/tensions that may affect operations
  • Determine whether the impact concerns a production territory or a sales territory
  • Map the internal actors (interests and power levers) and external actors (interests and power levers) of identified crises

Step 4: Analyze Activity Flows

  • Identify crises/conflicts/tensions that may affect supply flows or sales flows
  • Assess whether the impact can be quantified
  • Map internal and external actors with their interests and power levers

Step 5: Visual Synthesis (Map or Diagram)

  • A diagram or map as a visual summary with an explicit legend
  • Show territories where conflicts occur, with illustration of the theatre and object of conflicts
  • Show internal actors with their main power levers (pictograms of varying size)
  • Show flows involved (inbound, outbound, with volume representation)
  • If space allows: external actors, power levers, other related conflicts

Step 6: Evaluate Data Reliability

Six-step data reliability evaluation:
  1. The source: has it proven reliable in the recent past?
  2. The author: who is it? Is the author credible?
  3. The date: is the information up to date?
  4. Our biases: what was I expecting to find?
  5. External validations: what can it be triangulated with?
  6. An accessible expert: the ultimate step in case of doubt (if budget and timeframe permit)
Example of reliability assessment:
A peer-reviewed article in a top-ranked journal (e.g., Technological Forecasting and Social Change) by established academics with cross-validations from other references scores high on reliability. A blog post by an anonymous activist or a report from a state-controlled media outlet (e.g., RT France) would score much lower and require expert validation.
