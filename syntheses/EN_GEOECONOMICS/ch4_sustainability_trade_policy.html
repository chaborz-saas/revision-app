Summary — Geoeconomics Ch 4: Sustainability, Trade Policy, and Geoeconomics

1. Companies as Responsible Geoeconomic Actors

1.1 Environment, Natural Resources, and Geopolitical Risk

  • Tensions over resources are exacerbated with climate change (Tamimi, 2017)
  • Economic activity is disrupted by natural disasters (De Villa et al., 2023)
  • Companies must integrate climate resilience into their strategy (Belhoste & Nivet, 2020)
  • Rising challenges related to energy sovereignty (Zamborsky et al., 2023)
Key insight: Climate change acts as a "threat multiplier" for geopolitical tensions. Resource scarcity (water, minerals, energy) intensifies rivalries and creates new geoeconomic stakes. Companies must factor environmental risks into their strategic planning alongside traditional geopolitical risks.

1.2 Social Responsibility and Territorial Acceptability

  • Social acceptability conditions the sustainability of territorial anchoring (Belhoste & Nivet, 2020)
  • Companies must integrate local and ethical issues (Tamimi, 2017)
  • Companies are increasingly expected to contribute to sustainable development (Belhoste & Dimitrova, 2023)
  • Requires a nuanced understanding of power relations and local identities (Behrendt & Khanna, 2021)
Territorial acceptability: The degree to which a company's presence and activities are accepted by the local community, institutions, and stakeholders in a given territory. Without social acceptability, companies face protests, regulatory obstacles, and reputational damage that threaten their long-term operations.

1.3 The Role of Multinationals in International Cooperation

  • MNCs can engage in multilateral processes and take part in conflict resolution or economic diplomacy (Behrendt & Khanna, 2021)
  • They can support global governance initiatives, with ties to public and private actors giving them a bridging role (Kobrin, 2015)
  • Commitments are sometimes criticized as instrumentalization (Tamimi, 2017) and potentially subject to controversy (Belhoste & Nivet, 2020)
  • Strengthened dialogue with NGOs and international organizations (Belhoste & Dimitrova, 2023)

1.4 Gray Zones: Territories Beyond Regulation

Gray zones: Areas with weak State governance where companies operate alongside risks of corruption, violence, and exploitation (De Villa et al., 2015). Companies face the complexity of operating across local, international, and informal norms (Caldarai & Iacovello, 2022).
  • Presence of companies in areas with weak State governance
  • Risks of corruption, violence, and exploitation (Tamimi, 2017)
  • Necessity for companies to adopt robust ethical policies (Belhoste & Nivet, 2020)
  • Must combine diplomacy, ethics, and performance (Belhoste & Dimitrova, 2023)

2. Free Trade, Protectionism, and Economic Rivalries

2.1 Trade Policies: An Overview

Trade policies = instruments through which governments influence international trade flows. They may aim to protect specific sectors, shape the terms of trade, or pursue non-economic objectives. Two kinds: free trade policies and protectionist policies.

2.2 Economic Arguments for Free Trade

  • Enhances efficiency by enabling optimal allocation of resources according to comparative advantage
  • Fosters innovation through exposure to international competition
  • Lower prices and a wider range of goods for consumers
  • Generates overall wealth growth, though gains are unevenly distributed
Theoretical basis: Free trade is grounded in theories of comparative advantage, particularly the Heckscher-Ohlin-Samuelson (HOS) model, which argues that countries should specialize in producing goods that use their abundant factors of production most efficiently.

2.3 Critiques of Free Trade

  • May lead to substantial sectoral losses in open economies
  • Can exacerbate social inequalities by weakening certain workers or regions
  • May create excessive dependence on foreign suppliers
  • Fails to account for environmental or social externalities

2.4 Economic Justifications for Protectionism

JustificationExplanation
Infant industry argumentTemporarily protecting a developing sector to allow it to become competitive before opening to international competition
Strategic trade policySupporting a national champion in a global oligopolistic sector to capture rents and build competitive advantages
Response to unfair practicesCountermeasures against foreign subsidies or dumping (selling below cost to destroy foreign competitors)
Non-economic objectivesSovereignty, national security, ecological transition, cultural preservation

2.5 The Ambiguous Effects of Protectionism

  • May generate overall welfare losses for the country applying it
  • Creates market distortions and may reduce incentives to innovate (though this is debated)
  • Can trigger trade retaliation and harm domestic exporters
  • Targeted protectionism: in certain contexts, may support long-term industrial strategy
The trade policy dilemma: Trade policies represent complex political choices between efficiency, equity, and sovereignty. There is no universally optimal policy; the right approach depends on a country's economic structure, geopolitical position, and strategic objectives.

2.6 Contemporary Issues: Reshoring and Economic Sovereignty

  • Revived interest in targeted protectionism with increasing geopolitical tensions
  • Implementation of reshoring strategies in sectors deemed critical (semiconductors, pharmaceuticals, defense)
  • Current debates about full free trade vs. strategic regionalism
  • Trade policies = complex political choices between efficiency, equity, and sovereignty

3. Sustainability and Geoeconomics

3.1 UN Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs)

SDGs: Adopted in 2015 by all UN Member States as part of the 2030 Agenda for Sustainable Development. They comprise 17 goals and 169 targets addressing poverty, inequality, climate change, environmental degradation, peace, and justice.
  • Aim to integrate economic growth, social inclusion, and environmental protection in a universal framework
  • Provide a shared roadmap for governments, firms, and civil society
  • Serve as both a normative agenda and a benchmarking tool for global and local policy evaluation

3.2 Integrating Sustainability into Geoeconomic Diagnostics

When formulating recommendations from a geoeconomic diagnostic, three potential axes of recommendation apply (from Session 3):

  1. Contractual mechanisms (force majeure clauses, risk-sharing)
  2. Geographic diversification of production and supply sites
  3. Dialogue with governments and local institutions
Two key questions for recommendations:
1. Regarding dialogue with governments: Do the recommendations align with free trade policies or protectionist policies?
2. Regarding all options: Do they align with the SDGs?

For exam purposes: Focus specifically on SDGs 2 (Zero Hunger), 6 (Clean Water and Sanitation), 7 (Affordable and Clean Energy), and 10 (Reduced Inequalities). For a comprehensive diagnostic, the full set of SDGs should be considered.

Integration example: A recommendation to diversify energy supply (geographic diversification strategy) supports SDG 7 (clean energy) if it involves renewable sources, while also addressing geoeconomic vulnerability to energy weaponization. If the new supply comes from developing nations, the recommendation should consider SDG 10 (reduced inequalities) to ensure fair trade conditions.
