Summary — HRM Ch 1: Introduction to HRM & Recruitment

1. What is Human Resources Management?

Definition (Peretti, 1977): Human Resources Management is all the activities that enable the company to have the human resources it needs, in the right quantity and quality.
Definition (Ferrary, 2014): HRM is a set of tools and practices that form a coherent managerial system whose objective is to implement the human dimension of the organization's strategy.

1.1 Vision of People at Work

Traditional HRM assumes a mechanistic vision of people at work: the individual is seen as conditionable and responsive to stimuli — essentially treated as a resource, comparable to a machine or another asset. This perspective places primary importance on HR practitioners and the tools they master.

1.3 Purposes of HRM

  • Select, retain, and train the employees the company needs for its strategic development
  • Contribute to effective, efficient, and creative collective action
  • Integrate employees' individual choices while maintaining a fair approach for the collective

2. HRM as a Shared Function

2.1 Main Actors

HRM is not solely the domain of the HR department. It is a shared function between:

  • HR Department — designs systems, manages policy, handles administration
  • Operational Managers — apply HR practices day-to-day within their teams

Other stakeholders include: General Management, employees themselves, employee representatives (internally); the State, trade unions, social security organizations, occupational health services, and external consultants (externally).

2.2 The Manager's HR Role

  • Participates in recruiting team members
  • Grants salary increases and bonuses
  • Evaluates the performance of team members
  • Proposes training opportunities
  • Supports career development
Key Principle: Activities vary in their distribution between HR and managers. For example, hiring decisions are exclusively the manager's responsibility (rating 5), while disciplinary notifications are exclusively HR's responsibility (rating 1). Identification of training needs and onboarding are equally shared (rating 3).

Historical Context: Early 20th-century workplaces (Taylorism) prioritized productivity over people, leading to repetitive, monotonous work. Workers were treated as cogs in a machine. The absence of employee advocacy reflected the pre-HR era. Over decades, the field evolved from simple personnel administration to a strategic partner role.

4. Ulrich Model of HR Roles (1997)

David Ulrich's model identifies four key roles for the HR function, organized along two axes: Future vs. Present orientation and Process vs. People focus.

5. HRM: Internal & External Adaptation

5.1 Internal Context

  • Mission / Purpose of the organization
  • Strategy (top-down vs. bottom-up)
  • Organizational structure
  • Corporate culture

5.2 Corporate Culture — Schein's Model

Corporate Culture (Schein): The set of fundamental assumptions that a given group has invented, discovered, or developed in learning to cope with problems of external adaptation and internal integration, that have worked well enough to be considered valid, and therefore to be taught to new members as the correct way to perceive, think, and feel.

Schein's model has three levels:

  1. Artifacts (Symbols): Visible aspects — symbols, stories, rituals
  2. Espoused Values: The values the organization discusses and claims to hold
  3. Fundamental Assumptions: Unquestioned beliefs taken for granted

5.3 External Context

  • Economic change: Consequences of globalization (work flexibility, diversity, top talents, skill-based approach); financialization impact on employees (lack of motivation)
  • Technological change: Being an actor of change; implementing new technologies (ICT, telework, AI); supporting new forms of work organization
  • Demographic change: New generations entering the workforce; aging population (senior employment, knowledge transfer)
  • Socio-cultural change: Corporate Social Responsibility (CSR)

6. Contemporary Challenges of HRM

  • New ways of working (distance/remote work)
  • New generations entering the labor market and the departure of seniors (knowledge transfer)
  • Transformation of contractual relationships in employment
  • Digitalization of organizations and the HR function
  • Diversity management
  • Ensuring employee well-being and health

7. The Recruitment Process

Definition: Recruitment is the process by which the company: (1) Identifies human resource needs, (2) Sources candidates, (3) Selects candidates, (4) Integrates new hires.

7.1 Identifying Needs — Job & Candidate Profiles

Job Profile includes: title and type of position, position within the organization, main duties and responsibilities, compensation, and career development prospects.

Candidate Profile includes: education (initial and ongoing), professional experience, skills (technical and behavioral), and specific characteristics (availability, geographical mobility).

Auclair Case Study — Head of Checkout/Reception: Job profile included managing 52 people (48 cashiers, 4 receptionists), reporting to Hypermarket Director, salary around 40,000 euros gross/year. Candidate profile required minimum BTS-level education, 5+ years team management experience, leadership and customer service soft skills.

7.2 Digital Recruitment & Employer Branding

  • Templates and pre-formatted job descriptions in ATS (Applicant Tracking System)
  • Developing employer branding: identify strengths/weaknesses honestly, position communication messages on attractive and truthful elements, deploy messages in various media (text, video) aligned with brand guidelines

Types of tests: Psychometric tests (measure specific aptitudes), Psychological tests (identify personality), Situational tests (assess ability to perform a given task). Limitations include questionable validity, static nature, limited scientific foundation, and questionable reliability.

7.6 Interview Biases

Recruiter-side biases:
  • Halo effect: One positive/negative trait influences overall impression
  • Projection bias: Recruiter projects own values onto candidate
  • Inference effect: Drawing unwarranted conclusions from limited information
Candidate-side bias:
  • Social desirability bias: Candidate presents an overly favorable image

7.7 Decision-Making

Recruitment decision-making is not purely rational. It follows Herbert Simon's concept of bounded rationality: decisions are made with limited information, time, and cognitive capacity. Harris and Brewster (1999) describe the "coffee machine system" — informal conversations that influence hiring decisions beyond formal procedures.

AI in Recruitment: While AI can help screen candidates, it raises important ethical questions about bias, fairness, and transparency. Algorithmic bias can perpetuate existing discrimination patterns.

8. Onboarding

8.1 Challenges of Onboarding

  • Successful recruitment depends on effective integration
  • Controlling turnover: 36% of new permanent hires leave within a year
  • Significant financial stakes
  • Transmitting culture and know-how

8.2 Supporting New Employees

  • Inform: Welcome booklet, training, onboarding sessions
  • Social Integration: Joining teams, social spaces, events
  • Psychological Adaptation: Mentor, colleagues
  • Learning Behaviors: Feedback from supervisors, interactions with experienced employees

Auclair Case Study — Recruitment Decision: In the context of replacing a dismissed department manager (Josette Boulard), the analysis considered three candidates (Emilie, Julien, Sylvie). Julien was recommended due to his reliability, consensus-building nature, and the fact that appointing a man in a female-dominated sector supports diversity objectives. Recruitment decisions must never be based on discriminatory criteria (union membership, pregnancy, age).
