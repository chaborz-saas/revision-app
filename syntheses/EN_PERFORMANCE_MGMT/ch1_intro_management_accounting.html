Summary — Performance Management Ch 1: Introduction to Management Accounting

1. Purpose of a Business

Purpose of a business: To create and keep a customer. The primary financial goal is to enhance the wealth of its owners in the long term.

Businesses must balance multiple objectives: profitability, employee welfare, environmental responsibility, product safety, and fair trading with suppliers. There is always a trade-off between risk and return.

2. Business Organisation

Most businesses are limited companies. Shareholders appoint a board of directors to:

  1. Set the overall direction for the business
  2. Monitor and control activities
  3. Communicate with shareholders and other stakeholders

Businesses are generally organised into functional departments (e.g. finance, marketing, production) or divisions based on geographical or product lines.

3. Strategic Management

Five steps of strategic management:
  1. Establish mission, vision and objectives — mission = overriding purpose; vision = aspirations; objectives = specific, often quantified targets
  2. Position analysis (e.g. SWOT analysis)
  3. Identify and assess strategic options
  4. Select strategic options and formulate plans — operational plans with specific actions, financial plans (budgets and forecasts)
  5. Perform, review and control — implement plans, monitor actual vs planned, take corrective action

4. Management Accounting — Role and Principles

Management accounting: Provides information to support internal decision-making, planning, control, and performance evaluation. It is future-oriented, detail-focused, and customisable for specific management needs.

Management accounting provides a service to managers by acting as:

  • Information provider — budgets, cost reports
  • Advisor — strategic guidance
  • Controller — ensuring fiscal discipline
  • Partner — collaborates with departments

Four Key Principles

  1. Influence — information provides insights and influences decision-making
  2. Relevance — information must be relevant to the decisions being made (financial and non-financial)
  3. Analysis — provides insights into a variety of options and cause-and-effect relationships
  4. Trust — managers must be able to trust the information to be confident in decision-making

Cost-Benefit of Information

Information should only be produced where the benefits exceed the costs of preparing it. Information should be relevant, timely, forward-looking, detailed, and adaptable.

5. Management Decisions

Managers use accounting information when deciding to:

  • Develop new products or services
  • Change prices or quantities
  • Outsource activities
  • Change operating capacity
  • Change purchasing, production, or distribution methods

Non-Financial Information

Examples (airline context): passenger volumes, load factors, market share, turnaround times, flight punctuality, cancellations, customer satisfaction, employee satisfaction, percentage of online bookings, maintenance hours per aircraft.

6. The Changing Business Landscape

Key changes: growth of the service sector, new industries, e-commerce, automated and lean manufacturing, greater product innovation, growth of outsourcing, faster response times.

Changing Role of the Management Accountant

  • More time spent on analysis and business advice
  • Key member of the management team — requires interpersonal, leadership, and management skills
  • Broader role as information manager within the business
  • Providing non-financial information has become increasingly important

7. Ethics in Management Accounting

Four ethical standards:
  • Integrity — communicate truthfully
  • Objectivity — avoid bias
  • Confidentiality — protect sensitive information
  • Competence — maintain professional skill
