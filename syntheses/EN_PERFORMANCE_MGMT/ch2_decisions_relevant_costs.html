Summary — Performance Management Ch 2: Decisions and Relevant Costs

1. Cost-Benefit Analysis

Cost-benefit analysis: A careful weighing of costs and benefits of plans and decisions. For a decision to be worthwhile, the benefits should exceed the associated costs.

Not all costs and benefits can be measured in monetary terms. Incremental changes (e.g. extending a product range) are easier to analyse than transformative changes (e.g. altering strategic direction).

2. Relevant vs Irrelevant Costs

Relevant costs must satisfy three conditions:
  1. Relate to the objective being pursued
  2. Be a future cost
  3. Vary with the decision (differential)

Types of Relevant Costs

Opportunity cost: The value of the next best alternative forgone. It does not involve an outlay of cash but represents a loss of potential increase in wealth.

Differential future outlay cost: A future cash expenditure that differs between alternatives.

Types of Irrelevant Costs

  • Sunk (past) costs — costs already incurred; cannot be recovered regardless of the decision
  • Committed costs — future costs arising from irrevocable decisions (e.g. legally binding contracts)
  • Non-differential future outlay costs — costs that remain the same regardless of the decision
Example — Car at a garage:
A garage bought a car for £9,000 (sunk cost). Without new tyres it can be sold for £10,500. New tyres cost £400.
Minimum selling price with tyres = £10,500 + £400 = £10,900
The £9,000 purchase price is irrelevant (sunk cost). The £10,500 is the opportunity cost (sale value forgone).

3. Relevant Cost of Labour

Decision framework for labour costs:
Spare capacity: Relevant cost = £0 (workers are paid regardless)
Full capacity — hire new staff: Relevant cost = wage rate of new employee
Full capacity — redeploy existing staff: Relevant cost = opportunity cost (revenue lost from other work)
Example — Engine replacement:
Reconditioned engine costs £1,200. Mechanic takes 7 hours at £15/hour.
Spare capacity: Labour cost = £0 → total = £12,100
Hire casual mechanic: Labour cost = £105 (7 × £15) → total = £12,205
Redeploy from work billed at £60/hr: Labour cost = £420 (7 × £60) → total = £12,520

4. Relevant Cost of Materials

Decision framework for material costs:
Not in inventory: Relevant cost = current purchase price
In inventory, in regular use: Relevant cost = current replacement cost
In inventory, no other use, can sell: Relevant cost = net realisable (sale) value
In inventory, no other use, cannot sell: Relevant cost = £0 (or savings on disposal costs)
In inventory, can substitute for another material: Relevant cost = cost savings from substitution

5. Non-Measurable Costs and Benefits

Some costs and benefits cannot be reliably quantified but should still inform decisions. Managers must use judgement. Examples include:

  • Customer dissatisfaction from turning away work
  • Reputation damage from using lower-quality casual labour
  • Uncertain future demand
  • Benefits beyond the immediate transaction (e.g. attracting more customers)

6. Decision Framework Summary

Steps for relevant cost analysis:
  1. Identify all costs associated with the decision
  2. Eliminate irrelevant costs (sunk, committed, non-differential)
  3. Determine opportunity costs
  4. Calculate differential future outlay costs
  5. Sum all relevant costs to find the minimum acceptable price or to evaluate the decision
  6. Consider non-quantifiable factors
