Summary — Performance Management Ch 4: Full Costing

1. What Is Full (Absorption) Costing?

Full (absorption) cost: The total amount of resources sacrificed to achieve a particular objective. It includes all costs: both variable costs and fixed costs (direct and indirect).

Uses of Full Cost Information

  • Pricing and output decisions — setting prices that cover all costs
  • Exercising control — assessing actual costs to find savings
  • Assessing relative efficiency — comparing costs across locations, methods
  • Assessing performance — measuring profit by comparing revenues and full costs

2. Single-Product Businesses (Process Costing)

Cost per Unit = Total Cost of Output / Number of Units Produced

Work in Progress (WIP)

Equivalent Units = Completed Units + (WIP Units × % Completion)

Cost per Equivalent Unit = Total Cost / Equivalent Units
Example — Gnome Sweet Gnome Ltd:
120 completed gnomes + 15 at 80% complete = 120 + 12 = 132 equivalent units
Total cost = £5,280.
Cost per gnome = £5,280 / 132 = £40
Cost of completed production = 120 × £40 = £4,800
Cost of WIP = 12 × £40 = £480

3. Multi-Product Businesses (Job Costing)

Direct cost: Can be identified with a specific cost unit (e.g. labour of a mechanic on a specific repair).
Indirect cost (overheads): Cannot be identified with a particular cost unit (e.g. rent of the garage).

Full (absorption) cost = Direct Cost + Indirect Cost (Overheads)
Key distinction: Direct/indirect is NOT the same as variable/fixed. A cost can be direct and fixed, or indirect and variable. The classification depends on whether the cost can be traced to a specific cost unit.

4. Overhead Recovery (Absorption) Rates

Overhead Recovery Rate = Total Overheads / Total Basis Units

Common bases:
• Direct labour hours (most common, logical for labour-intensive operations)
• Machine hours (best for machine-intensive operations)
• Percentage of direct labour cost
Example — Johnson Ltd:
Overheads = £10,000/month. Direct labour hours = 1,000/month.
Overhead rate = £10,000 / 1,000 = £10 per direct labour hour
For a repair using 3 hours: Direct materials £15 + Direct labour £48 + Overheads (3 × £10) £30 = Full cost £93

Why Direct Labour Hours?

  • Time is a rough measure of job size
  • Overheads are largely time-based (rent, depreciation, salaries)
  • Labour hours are already measured for payroll purposes — practical to apply

5. Cost Centres

Cost centre: A distinct area, activity, or function for which costs are separately determined.
Product (production) cost centres: Departments where jobs are worked on.
Service cost centres: Departments that support production (e.g. HR, maintenance).

Steps for Handling Overheads on a Cost Centre Basis

  1. Allocate overheads specific to each cost centre (e.g. department salaries, separately metered electricity)
  2. Apportion shared overheads to cost centres using a fair basis (e.g. floor area for rent, number of employees for HR)
  3. Reapportion service cost centre overheads to product cost centres
  4. Calculate an overhead recovery rate for each product cost centre
  5. Absorb overheads into jobs/units based on their usage of each cost centre

Common Apportionment Bases

Overhead TypeApportionment Basis
Rent, rates, heating, lightingFloor area (sqm)
Machine depreciation, maintenanceMachine hours or carrying amount
Canteen, HR servicesNumber of employees
Insurance of buildingsFloor area
Equipment insuranceCarrying amount of equipment

6. Batch Costing

Cost per Unit = Cost of the Batch / Number of Units in the Batch

7. Full Costing vs Variable (Marginal) Costing

FeatureFull (Absorption) CostingVariable (Marginal) Costing
Product cost includesAll production costs (variable + fixed)Only variable production costs
Fixed manufacturing overheadsAbsorbed into inventoryTreated as a period expense
Inventory valuationHigher (includes fixed overheads)Lower (variable costs only)
Income statement formatShows gross profitShows contribution margin
Use in financial accountingRequired by standards (IAS 2)Not compliant for external reporting
Decision makingCan obscure CVP relationshipsHighlights cost-volume-profit relationships

Effect of Inventory Changes on Profit

When inventory increases (production > sales): Absorption costing profit > Variable costing profit
When inventory decreases (sales > production): Absorption costing profit < Variable costing profit
When inventory is unchanged: Both methods give the same profit

Difference = Change in inventory units × Fixed overhead per unit
Reconciliation:
Variable costing profit
+ Fixed overhead in closing inventory
- Fixed overhead in opening inventory
= Absorption costing profit
Example — Lahore Ltd (June):
Production 6,000, Sales 4,000. Variable cost £5/unit. Fixed overhead £18,000 (£3/unit). Selling price £14.
Variable costing profit: Contribution (4,000 × £9) - Fixed costs £23,000 = £13,000
Absorption costing profit: Gross profit (4,000 × £6) - Non-mfg costs £5,000 = £19,000
Difference = 2,000 units increase × £3 = £6,000
