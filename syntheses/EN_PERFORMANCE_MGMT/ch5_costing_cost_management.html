Summary — Performance Management Ch 5: Costing and Cost Management in a Competitive Environment

1. Traditional vs. New Costing Environment

Traditional environment: Production was direct-labour-intensive and labour-paced. Overheads were low relative to direct costs. Markets were relatively uncompetitive.
Modern environment: Production is capital-intensive and machine-paced. Overheads are high relative to direct costs. Markets are highly competitive and international. Product life cycles are shorter, making the design stage critical for cost management.

Traditional Job Costing

Full cost of a unit = Direct costs + Indirect costs (overheads). Overheads are allocated or apportioned to cost centres, then charged to output using overhead absorption rates. This is Full (Absorption) Costing.

2. Activity-Based Costing (ABC)

ABC treats all overhead costs as being caused or "driven" by activities. It identifies support activities, collects their costs into cost pools, and uses cost drivers to absorb overheads to each unit of output. ABC provides more accurate product costing than the traditional approach.

Steps to Implement ABC

  1. Identify the support activities involved in making products or providing services
  2. Identify the costs of each support activity
  3. Identify the cost drivers that cause a change in costs of each activity
  4. Establish a cost pool for each support activity (one pool per cost driver)
  5. Assign costs to the relevant cost pool; establish total costs in each pool
  6. Calculate cost per unit of cost driver; absorb into units based on driver usage
Cost per unit of cost driver = Total cost pool / Total units of cost driver
Overhead charged to product = Cost per driver unit × Driver units used by that product
Example — Modern Producers Ltd: Storage area cost pool = £90,000. Product A: 50,000 units × 1 week = 50,000 product weeks. Product B: 25,000 units × 4 weeks = 100,000 product weeks. Total = 150,000 weeks. Cost per product week = £90,000 / 150,000 = £0.60. Product A absorbs £0.60 per unit; Product B absorbs £2.40 per unit.
Comma Ltd — Traditional vs. ABC:
Traditional approach: overhead rate = £333,200 / 54,000 DLH = £6.17/DLH. Standard RoS = 17.08%, Deluxe RoS = 20.52%.
ABC approach: Standard overhead/unit = £5.77, Deluxe = £22.00. Standard RoS = 25.29%, Deluxe = 14.55%.
Conclusion: ABC reveals the Standard is far more profitable. The business should reconsider pushing Deluxe.

ABC Benefits

  • More accurate cost figures per unit of output
  • Better pricing and product mix decisions
  • Improved understanding of the business and overhead cost behaviour
  • Helps identify ways to cut costs and improve performance

ABC Criticisms

  • Costly and time-consuming to set up, update, and run
  • Reports can be complex and difficult to interpret
  • May give similar results to traditional costing when products are similar or overheads are low
  • Not all costs can be easily traced to a particular activity
  • Cause-and-effect relationship between cost drivers and costs can be hard to establish

3. Total (Whole) Life-Cycle Costing

Total life-cycle costing tracks and reports all costs from the beginning to the end of a product's life. The three phases are: pre-production, production, and post-production.
A high proportion of costs is incurred and/or committed during the pre-production phase (R&D, design, set-up, advertising). Traditional costing mainly captures production phase costs.
PhaseActivities
Pre-productionR&D, product/service design, production facility set-up, advertising and promotion
ProductionManufacturing and selling; traditional absorption costing or ABC applied here
Post-productionAfter-sales service, warranty, fault correction, environmental clean-up, closure costs

4. Target Costing

Target costing is a market-based approach used at the pre-production phase. It starts from the target selling price, deducts the target profit, and derives the maximum allowable cost — the target cost.
Target Cost = Target Price − Target Profit

Steps in Target Costing

  1. Determine target price of the product
  2. Determine the target profit required
  3. Calculate target cost (target price − target profit)
  4. Estimate the current cost of the product
  5. Compare target cost with estimated cost
  6. If estimated cost exceeds target, find ways to reduce it
Potential problems: Can lead to conflict with suppliers/employees, stress among staff, delays in product launches, and high implementation costs.

5. Kaizen Costing

Kaizen costing focuses on continual and gradual cost reduction during the production phase. It sets periodic cost reduction targets and compares them with actual reductions achieved. It is part of a broader culture of continuous improvement.

Kaizen Steps

  1. Determine the current cost of the product
  2. Set cost reduction target for the next period
  3. Determine actual cost reduction achieved
  4. Compare target with actual cost reduction
  5. Where there is a significant variance, take appropriate action

Focus Areas for Waste Elimination

  • Eliminating overproduction
  • Reducing defective products through process changes
  • Synchronising manufacturing processes and eliminating bottlenecks
  • Identifying and eliminating excess inventories
  • Reducing waiting times through closer supplier links
  • Improving storage and handling processes
Relationship: Total lifecycle costing provides the framework. Target costing operates in the pre-production phase. Kaizen costing operates in the production phase. Together they cover the full product life.

6. Value Chain Analysis

Value chain analysis examines activities in the product life cycle to identify and try to eliminate non-value-added activities. Each link in the chain is an activity that incurs costs and should add value.

Porter's Value Chain

TypeActivities
Primary (5)Inbound logistics, Operations, Outbound logistics, Marketing & sales, Service
Support (4)HR management, Technology/R&D, Procurement, Admin/finance/QA
Each activity should add value (customer willing to pay more than its cost). A business must configure the value chain for either cost advantage or differentiation. Non-value-adding activities should be eliminated.

7. Benchmarking

Benchmarking is a continuing process of identifying a successful operation (within or outside the business) and attempting to emulate it. It aims to gain greater operating efficiency and competitive advantage.

Steps in Benchmarking

  1. Determine the product, process, or function to benchmark
  2. Identify a suitable benchmark (best-in-class)
  3. Select suitable measures for the benchmarking process
  4. Collect and analyse information relating to the measures
  5. Identify areas for improvement
  6. Introduce new practices and set appropriate targets
Example measures for benchmarking a taxi service: profit as % of fare income, fuel cost as % of fare income, average fare income per taxi, average running costs per taxi, percentage of calls answered within one minute, number of accidents per 1,000 miles.

8. Total Quality Management (TQM)

TQM is a philosophy concerned with providing products that meet or exceed customers' requirements all of the time. Quality costs are divided into four categories.
CategoryDescriptionExamples
Prevention costsCosts to prevent quality failuresTraining, new equipment, process design
Appraisal costsCosts of monitoring and inspecting qualityInspection of materials, WIP, finished goods; testing equipment
Internal failure costsCosts of defects found before deliveryReworking, scrap, re-testing, downgrading
External failure costsCosts of defects found after deliveryCustomer complaints, returns, recalls, lost reputation, legal liability
Key insight: If more is spent on prevention costs, savings can be made through reduced appraisal and failure costs. The goal is zero defects.

Steps for Quality Improvement

  1. Identify areas for quality improvement
  2. Design new processes to achieve zero defects
  3. Develop suitable measures for product quality
  4. Develop staff training and incentive schemes for quality
  5. Implement new processes and collect quality data
  6. Analyse information and take action for continuous improvement

9. Alternative View

Costs may be managed without sophisticated techniques if:

  • There is shared responsibility for managing costs
  • Discussion of costs is an everyday activity
  • Costs are managed locally
  • Benchmarking is used at regular intervals
  • The focus is on managing rather than just reducing costs
