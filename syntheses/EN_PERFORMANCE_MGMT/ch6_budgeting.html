Summary — Performance Management Ch 6: Budgeting

1. What is a Budget?

A budget is a short-term business plan, usually for one year with monthly sub-periods, expressed in financial or physical terms. Budgets are the short-term means of working towards the business's strategic objectives.
Budget vs. Forecast: A budget is a plan showing an intention or determination to achieve targets. A forecast is a prediction of the future state of the environment. Forecasts inform budgets, but they are distinctly different.

How Budgets Link with Strategy

  1. Establish mission, vision and objectives
  2. Undertake a position analysis (SWOT)
  3. Identify and assess strategic options
  4. Select strategic options and formulate plans
  5. Prepare budgets (cash, sales, inventories, labour, production, capital expenditure)
  6. Perform, review and control (identify variances, respond, revise plans)

2. Types of Budgets

Periodic vs. Continual (Rolling) Budgets

Periodic budgets: prepared for a particular period, usually one year.
Continual (rolling) budgets: continually updated, ensuring there are always budgets for a full planning period ahead.

Incremental vs. Zero-Base Budgeting (ZBB)

FeatureIncrementalZero-Base (ZBB)
Starting pointPrevious period's budget + adjustmentZero — all spending must be justified
ApproachAdjusts for known changesQuestioning approach; every item challenged
AdvantageSimpler, quickerMore efficient use of resources
RiskPerpetuates past inefficienciesMore time-consuming

3. Purposes (Uses) of Budgets

  • Promote forward thinking — forces managers to plan ahead
  • Help coordinate the various aspects of the business
  • Motivate performance — provide targets
  • Provide a system of control — compare actual vs. budget (variance analysis)
  • Provide a system of authorisation — set spending limits

4. Limiting Factors

A limiting factor (also called a key factor or binding constraint) restricts the business's ability to achieve its objectives (e.g., sales demand, labour, materials, plant capacity). Budgets should start with the limiting factor because all other budgets depend on it.

5. The Budget-Setting Process

  1. Establish who will take responsibility
  2. Communicate budget guidelines
  3. Identify the key (limiting) factor
  4. Prepare budget for key factor area
  5. Prepare draft budgets for all other areas
  6. Review and coordinate
  7. Prepare master budgets (budgeted income statement and statement of financial position)
  8. Communicate budgets to interested parties
  9. Monitor performance relative to budget

6. Master Budget Structure

The master budget comprises interlinked budgets:

  • Sales budget (usually the starting point / limiting factor)
  • Production budget (derived from sales + inventory changes)
  • Materials, labour, overheads budgets
  • Finished goods & raw materials inventories budgets
  • Trade receivables & payables budgets
  • Capital expenditure budget
  • Cash budget (synthesis of all cash inflows and outflows)

7. The Cash Budget

The cash budget shows planned cash receipts and payments, and the running cash balance. It is essential for identifying potential cash shortages or surpluses.

Structure of a Cash Budget

  1. Break the budget period into sub-periods (months)
  2. Use columnar form, one column per month
  3. Identify cash receipts under various headings; show monthly totals
  4. Identify cash payments under various headings; show monthly totals
  5. Calculate surplus/deficit for each month (receipts − payments)
  6. Calculate running cash balance (previous balance +/− surplus/deficit)
Key rules: Depreciation is NOT a cash flow. Credit terms determine the timing lag between revenue/expense recognition and cash receipt/payment. Inventory changes affect the amount of purchases needed.
Purchases = Cost of goods sold + Closing inventory − Opening inventory
Closing cash balance = Opening balance + Cash receipts − Cash payments
Example 6.1: Business with £12,000 opening cash. Sales on one month's credit. Purchases on one month's credit. Depreciation £3,000/month excluded from cash budget. Van purchase in March: cost £15,000 − £4,000 trade-in = £11,000 net payment. Electricity paid quarterly in arrears. Cash balance grows from £12,000 in January to £60,000 by end of June.

8. Supporting Budgets

Trade Receivables Budget

Closing receivables = Opening receivables + Credit sales − Cash receipts from customers

Trade Payables Budget

Closing payables = Opening payables + Credit purchases − Cash payments to suppliers

Inventories Budget

Closing inventory = Opening inventory + Purchases (or production) − Usage (or sales at cost)

9. Activity-Based Budgeting (ABB)

ABB extends the principles of Activity-Based Costing to budgeting. Budgets are based on cost-driving activities rather than business functions, potentially resulting in more accurate budgets.

Steps in ABB

  1. Determine sales budget
  2. Identify activities needed to achieve the sales budget
  3. Identify cost drivers for each activity
  4. Calculate the cost driver rate
  5. Estimate total usage of the cost driver based on the sales budget
  6. Multiply estimated total usage by the cost driver rate
Example — Danube Ltd: Gamma (60,000 units × 2 weeks) + Delta (80,000 units × 5 weeks) = 520,000 product weeks. At £1.50 per product week, the activity budget for finished goods stores = £780,000.

10. Criticisms of Conventional Budgeting

  • Cannot deal with rapid change
  • Focus on short-term financial targets, not value creation
  • Encourage a "top-down" management style
  • Can be time-consuming and costly
  • Based around traditional functions; do not cross boundaries
  • Encourage incremental thinking ("last year + x%")
  • Protect rather than lower costs
  • Promote "sharp" practice among managers (budget gaming)

11. Beyond Budgeting

Beyond Budgeting is a broader, more integrated approach to planning that replaces the traditional budget with rolling forecasts, KPIs, decentralised decision-making, and a questioning culture.

Key Features

  • Leaner financial planning process
  • Rolling budgets and KPIs (monetary and non-monetary targets)
  • Decentralised, participative approach
  • Network structure rather than traditional hierarchy
  • Decision-making devolved to "front-line" managers
  • Sharing of knowledge and best practice
  • Rewards linked to targets based on performance improvement
  • Greater adaptability to changing conditions
Despite criticisms, budgeting is still widely regarded as useful and extensively practised. The future may see a looser, more dynamic form incorporating rolling plans with moving targets.
