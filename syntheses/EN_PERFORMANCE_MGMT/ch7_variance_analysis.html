Summary — Performance Management Ch 7: Standard Costing and Variance Analysis

1. Budgetary Control Process

Budgetary control involves: (1) Prepare budgets, (2) Perform activities, (3) Collect information on actuals, (4) Identify variances between budgeted and actual performance, (5) Respond to variances and exercise control.

Types of Control

  • Feedback control: Things have gone wrong; steps are taken to get back on track (reactive)
  • Feedforward control: Predicts what could go wrong; steps are taken to avoid problems (proactive)

2. Flexed Budgets

Flexing a budget means revising the budget to reflect the actual volume of output. This makes the budget comparable to actual performance, since comparing a budget for 1,000 units with actuals for 900 units would be misleading.
Flexed budget figure = Original budget figure × (Actual output / Budgeted output)
Variable costs and revenues are flexed; fixed costs remain unchanged.
The flexed budget is essential for meaningful variance analysis. Without it, you cannot distinguish between volume effects and efficiency/price effects.

3. Variance Analysis Overview

Actual Profit = Budgeted Profit + Favourable Variances − Adverse Variances
Variance TypeSub-variances
SalesVolume variance, Price variance
Direct MaterialsUsage variance, Price variance
Direct LabourEfficiency variance, Rate variance
Fixed OverheadsSpending (expenditure) variance

4. Sales Variances

Sales Volume Variance

Sales Volume Variance = Flexed budget profit − Original budget profit
Favourable if flexed profit > original profit (sold more than planned)

Sales Price Variance

Sales Price Variance = Actual revenue − (Actual units × Budget selling price)
Favourable if actual revenue > expected revenue at budget price (sold at a higher price)
Baxter Ltd — May: Budget: 1,000 units at £10. Actual: 900 units at £92,000 total.
Volume variance: £16,000 − £20,000 = £4,000 Adverse.
Price variance: £92,000 − (900 × £10) = £92,000 − £90,000 = £2,000 Favourable.

5. Direct Materials Variances

Materials Usage Variance

Usage Variance = (Standard qty for actual output − Actual qty) × Standard price
Favourable if actual quantity used < standard quantity

Materials Price Variance

Price Variance = (Actual qty × Standard price) − Actual cost
Favourable if actual cost < expected cost at standard price
Baxter May: Standard: 40m per unit at £1/m. Actual: 900 units used 37,000m costing £36,900.
Usage: (36,000 − 37,000) × £1 = £1,000 Adverse (used 1,000m more than expected).
Price: (37,000 × £1) − £36,900 = £100 Favourable (paid less per metre).

6. Direct Labour Variances

Labour Efficiency Variance

Efficiency Variance = (Standard hours for actual output − Actual hours) × Standard rate
Favourable if actual hours < standard hours (worked faster)

Labour Rate Variance

Rate Variance = (Actual hours × Standard rate) − Actual labour cost
Favourable if actual cost < expected cost at standard rate
Baxter May: Standard: 1.25 hrs/unit at £16/hr. Actual: 900 units took 1,075 hrs costing £17,500.
Efficiency: (1,125 − 1,075) × £16 = £800 Favourable.
Rate: (1,075 × £16) − £17,500 = £17,200 − £17,500 = £300 Adverse.

7. Fixed Overhead Variance

Fixed Overhead Spending Variance = Budgeted fixed overheads − Actual fixed overheads
Favourable if actual < budget (spent less than planned)
Fixed overheads are NOT flexed because they do not change with volume. The variance is simply the difference between the original budget and the actual amount spent.

8. Profit Reconciliation

Baxter May Reconciliation:
Budgeted operating profit: £20,000
Favourable: Sales price +£2,000, Materials price +£100, Labour efficiency +£800 = +£2,900
Adverse: Sales volume −£4,000, Materials usage −£1,000, Labour rate −£300, Fixed OH −£700 = −£6,000
Actual operating profit: £16,900

9. Causes of Variances

VariancePossible Causes
Sales volume (adverse)Poor sales performance, market deterioration, production shortages
Sales price (adverse)Price competition, poor negotiation, market decline
Materials usage (adverse)Poor production performance, substandard materials, faulty machinery
Materials price (adverse)Poor buying performance, higher quality material, market price increases
Labour efficiency (adverse)Poor supervision, low skill workers, material problems, machine downtime
Labour rate (adverse)Using higher grade workers, pay increases, overtime premiums
Fixed OH spending (adverse)Poor supervision of overheads, general cost increases not budgeted

10. Investigating Variances

  • Significant and/or persistent variances should be investigated
  • Costs and benefits of investigating must be considered
  • Trading off favourable against adverse variances should not be automatic
  • Cumulative variances can reveal patterns not visible in individual periods
Compensating variances: Linked favourable and adverse variances that offset each other (e.g., lower price → higher volume). These should be examined carefully rather than simply netted off.

11. Standard Costing

Standards are budgeted physical quantities and financial values for one unit of input/output. They can be ideal standards (perfect conditions) or practical standards (achievable but challenging).

Uses of Standards

  • Performance evaluation and control
  • Measuring operating efficiency
  • Product-sourcing decisions
  • Inventory and WIP costing for income measurement
  • Pricing decisions

Problems with Standard Costing

  • Standards can quickly become out of date
  • Managers may be held responsible for factors beyond their control
  • No incentive to improve beyond the standard
  • Can create perverse incentives (e.g., building excess inventories)

13. Behavioural Issues

  • Budgets can improve job satisfaction by bringing structure and certainty
  • Demanding yet achievable targets motivate best
  • Unrealistically demanding targets lead to declining performance
  • Once targets seem unachievable, motivation and performance decline
