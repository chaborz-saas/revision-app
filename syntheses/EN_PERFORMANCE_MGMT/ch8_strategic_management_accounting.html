Summary — Performance Management Ch 8: Strategic Management Accounting

Part A — Strategic Management Accounting (Chapter 10)

1. What is Strategic Management Accounting (SMA)?

SMA is concerned with providing information to support strategic plans and decisions. It is outward-looking (considering external factors), focused on outperforming competition, and concerned with monitoring progress towards strategic objectives.

Strategic Planning Steps

  1. Establish mission, vision and objectives
  2. Undertake a position analysis (e.g., SWOT)
  3. Identify and assess strategic options
  4. Select the most appropriate strategic options and formulate plans
  5. Review performance and exercise control

2. Competitor Analysis

Competitor analysis examines the objectives, strategies, assumptions and resource capabilities of competitors. The purpose is to gain a source of competitive advantage.

Four Aspects of Competitor Analysis

AspectKey Questions
1. Mission & ObjectivesWhat are their profit, sales growth, market share objectives?
2. StrategiesInvestments in technology, alliances, products, M&A, cost reduction?
3. AssumptionsWhat do they assume about industry trends, competitive strengths, new markets?
4. Resources & CapabilitiesScale, technology, profitability, cost structure, management quality, reputation?

3. Customer Profitability Analysis (CPA)

CPA assesses the profitability of each customer or type of customer. It recognises that much of the cost incurred is not related to the cost of products sold, but to the cost of selling and distribution.

Customer-Related Cost Categories

  • Handling orders — receiving orders, processing, dispatch
  • Visiting customers — sales employee visits
  • Delivering goods — transport, logistics
  • Holding inventories — storage costs for customer orders
  • Granting credit — financing credit terms
  • After-sales support — technical assistance, servicing
Key insight: Trend analysis can show whether customer profitability problems are one-offs or arise consistently over time. High-revenue customers may actually be unprofitable once all customer-related costs are considered.

4. The Balanced Scorecard

The balanced scorecard is a management tool that uses both financial and non-financial measures to assess progress towards objectives, organised across four perspectives.
PerspectiveExample Measures
FinancialROCE, operating profit margin, % sales revenue growth
CustomerCustomer satisfaction, new customer growth levels
Internal Business Processes% sales from new products, time to market, product cycle times, response times to complaints
Learning & GrowthEmployee motivation, employee skills profiles, information systems capabilities
The balanced scorecard translates strategy into operational processes. There is a cause-and-effect relationship: learning & growth improves internal processes, which improves customer satisfaction, which drives financial performance.

5. Measuring Shareholder Value — EVA

Problems with Accounting Profit

  1. Measured over a relatively short period (usually one year)
  2. Risk is ignored
  3. Does not account for all capital costs
  4. Can vary according to accounting policies adopted
Economic Value Added (EVA) measures whether the returns generated by the business exceed the required returns of investors.
EVA = NOPAT − (R × C)
Where:
• NOPAT = Net Operating Profit After Tax (adjusted)
• R = Required rate of return from investors (cost of capital)
• C = Capital invested (net assets of the business)

EVA Adjustments

ItemAdjustment
Marketing costs (long-term benefit)Add back to NOPAT the portion not yet expired (capitalise and amortise)
Excess allowances for receivablesAdd back the excessive portion to NOPAT
Restructuring costsTreated as investment, not expense (add back to NOPAT)
Marketable investmentsNot included in capital invested (not part of business operations)
Peverell plc — EVA Calculation:
Operating profit: £38.0m
Add back: Marketing (4/5 × £40m) = £32.0m, Excess allowance = £8.0m
Adjusted operating profit = £78.0m
Tax (20%) = £15.6m
Adjusted NOPAT = £62.4m
EVA = £62.4m − (12% × £150m) = £62.4m − £18.0m = £44.4m

6. Pricing Strategies

Profit is maximised where: Marginal Revenue = Marginal Cost

Pricing Methods

MethodApproachLimitations
Full cost (cost-plus)Full cost + profit mark-upMarket may not accept; ignores demand
Marginal costMarginal cost + mark-upMust cover all costs in long run
Target pricingMarket price sets ceiling; cost adjustedMarket-driven; cost management needed

Pricing Strategies

StrategyDescription
Penetration pricingNew product priced low to gain market share and discourage competitors; price raised once established
Price skimmingNew product priced high initially for price-insensitive customers; price gradually lowered as each market stratum is saturated
Price elasticity of demand: How sensitive demand is to price changes. Elastic = demand changes significantly with price; Inelastic = demand changes little with price.

Part B — Divisionalisation (Chapter 11)

7. Responsibility Centres

TypeManager Responsible For
Cost centreManaging costs
Profit centreManaging revenues and costs
Investment centreManaging revenues, costs, and investment

Benefits of Divisionalisation

  • Better access to market information
  • Motivating middle and junior managers
  • Developing junior managers through experience
  • Better use of specialised knowledge
  • Allowing senior managers to focus on strategic issues
  • Enabling timely decision making

Problems of Divisionalisation

  • Goal conflict between divisions
  • Excessive risk avoidance
  • Excessive management perks
  • Costly duplication and loss of economies of scale
  • Divisions competing to detriment of the whole business

8. Divisional Performance Measurement

Return on Investment (ROI)

ROI = (Divisional Profit / Divisional Investment) × 100%
ROI problem: A division may reject a project that exceeds the cost of capital because it would lower the division's existing ROI. This leads to sub-optimal decision-making for the business as a whole.

Residual Income (RI)

RI = Divisional Profit − (Investment × Cost of Capital)
Goodman Division:
Profit = £25,000. Investment = £150,000. Cost of capital = 20%.
ROI = 25,000 / 150,000 = 16.7% (below 20% target).
RI = 25,000 − (20% × 150,000) = 25,000 − 30,000 = −£5,000 (negative = unsatisfactory).

Divisional Profit Measures

  1. Contribution — sales minus variable costs
  2. Controllable profit — only costs within the manager's control
  3. Divisional profit before common expenses — controllable and non-controllable costs
  4. Divisional profit after common expenses — after share of overheads

9. Transfer Pricing

Transfer pricing involves setting prices for internal transfers (sales and purchases) between divisions of the same business. It directly affects divisional profit and therefore ROI and RI.

Objectives of Transfer Pricing

  • Promote divisional independence (autonomy)
  • Provide a basis for measuring effectiveness
  • Promote the objectives of the business as a whole
  • Allocate resources to divisions
  • Minimise tax charges (in international context)
The best transfer prices are based on opportunity cost for both the buying and selling divisions.

Transfer Pricing Methods

MethodDescriptionPros/Cons
Market priceBased on external market priceUsually best (= opportunity cost), but market may not exist
Variable costBased on variable cost of productionRepresents opportunity cost with spare capacity; no profit for selling division
Full cost (+ loading)Full cost plus profit marginRarely reflects opportunity cost; passes on inefficiencies
Negotiated priceAgreed between divisionsPromotes independence but can be unfair
Key principle: If the selling division has spare capacity, the minimum transfer price = variable cost. If operating at full capacity, the minimum = market price (since it must forgo external sales). The buying division's maximum = price of cheapest acceptable external supplier.

10. Non-Financial Measures

Non-financial measures have increased in importance due to environmental uncertainty. They should be integrated with financial measures using a framework such as the balanced scorecard.

Areas for Non-Financial Measurement

  • Research and development
  • Staff training and morale
  • Product/service quality
  • Market share
  • Environmental and social concerns
