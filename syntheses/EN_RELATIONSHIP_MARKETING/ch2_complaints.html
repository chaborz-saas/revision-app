Summary — Relationship Marketing Ch2: Complaints — Last Chance Relationships

Complaints are a direct expression of dissatisfaction. This chapter covers how to manage them as a last-chance opportunity to restore customer loyalty and as a tool for continuous improvement.

1. Costs of Dissatisfaction 2. Complaints Process 3. Compensation Grid 4. Corrective & Preventive 5. Complaints Database 6. KPIs & Pareto 7. Challenges

1. The Cost of Customer Dissatisfaction

Complaints are a direct expression of dissatisfaction, critical for loyalty and ultimately profit. They sit in the chain: Quality Problem → Dissatisfaction → Loyalty?? → Profit???

1.1 Direct Costs

  • Cost of warranties and indemnities
  • Cost of handling complaints

1.2 Opportunity Costs

  • Lost revenue from customers who no longer buy (whether they complained or not)
  • Dissatisfied people who dissuade potential customers from buying (negative word-of-mouth)
Total Cost of Dissatisfaction = Direct Costs + Opportunity Costs
Example of calculating the full cost of a complaint (B2B):
Internal time (collection, analysis, solution): 4 hours x 30 EUR/hour = 120 EUR
Travel + meeting: 2 hours
Meal invitation: 30 EUR
Special delivery: 500 EUR
Exceptional discount: 300 EUR
Total: 950 EUR for a single complaint

2. The Complaints Management Process

The complaints management process is the sequence of activities that restores the enthusiasm of the dissatisfied customer. It also triggers corrective actions to protect other customers.

The 5-step process:

StepDescription
1. Collection & CentralizationGather all complaints from all channels into a single system
2. Analysis & ValidationInvestigate the complaint, determine if justified
3. Providing ResponseCommunicate the solution to the customer
4. Corrective ActionFix the specific customer's problem (Direct result)
5. Progress ActionsImplement systemic changes to prevent recurrence

2.1 The Complaint Form

A complaint form ensures accurate and structured data collection. It typically includes:

  • Customer identification and date
  • Description of the complaint
  • Justified? (Yes/No)
  • First customer contact details
  • Person responsible for handling
  • Analysis / Investigation results
  • Response provided to client
  • Compensation given
  • Verification of achievement
  • Root causes identified
  • Corrective actions taken

3. The Standard Compensation Grid

A standard compensation grid determines the appropriate response based on customer type (strategic, important, standard) and criticality (low, medium, high). It should be built collaboratively by Sales, Finance, and Quality departments.

4. Corrective vs. Preventive Actions

4.1 Two Types of Actions

1. Corrective actions: Fix the particular case of the customer who complains (product exchange, financial compensation, etc.).
2. Preventive actions: Remove root causes of dissatisfaction to prevent similar problems for all customers. May require questioning the organization, systems, skills, or supplier quality.

4.2 Root-Cause Analysis

The Ishikawa Diagram (Fishbone / Cause-and-Effect Diagram) is used to identify possible causes of a problem. Potential causes are searched in 6 standard domains called "the 6 Ms":
  1. Man (people, skills)
  2. Machine (equipment, tools)
  3. Method (processes, procedures)
  4. Material (raw materials, supplies)
  5. Measurement (inspection, quality control)
  6. Milieu/Environment (workplace, conditions)
Key insight: To prevent dissatisfaction, you often have to look for the cause of the cause of the cause! Root-cause analysis goes deep, not just surface-level fixes.

4.3 Securing Actions

When analysis shows a problem is frequent AND serious, launch securing actions:

  • Recall on products likely to be non-compliant
  • Stock sorting
  • Inform customers of a defect proactively

5. The Complaints Database

A Complaints Database centralizes all complaint forms to ensure traceability and enable tracking and analysis. It typically includes: complaint ID, date, customer name, product, object of complaint, customer journey stage, customer action, customer verbatim, cause, and corrective action.
Purpose: The database makes it possible to identify patterns, analyze root causes, and develop systemic corrective actions across the entire customer base.

6. Complaints KPIs & Pareto Analysis

6.1 Key KPIs to Monitor

  1. Evolution of complaints number over time
  2. Type of complaints (categories)
  3. Costs of complaints
  4. Effectiveness of treatment: customer feelings about the solution provided
  5. Response time: average processing duration, % responded within target time (e.g., 48h)

6.2 Pareto Analysis (The 80/20 Rule)

Pareto's Law: Approximately 20% of cause types generate 80% of consequences. Applied to complaints: a small number of complaint types account for the majority of all complaints.
Example: In one analysis, 2 cause types out of 7 (29%) accounted for 75% of all complaints (Delivery and Delay issues). Focusing preventive actions on these two areas has the highest impact.
Benefits of Pareto analysis: Visual and easy to implement, forces quantitative thinking, helps make the right decisions about where to invest prevention resources. Note: it is not a scientific law but a useful heuristic.

6.3 Attrition Rate

Attrition Rate = Number of customers lost over the period / Number of customers at the start of the period

7. Challenges of Complaint Management

7.1 Challenges for Companies

  • Setting up a genuine listening system to collect complaints
  • Demonstrating empathy and respect when facing customer anger or disappointment
  • Being ready to question the company's way of working

7.2 Challenges for Staff

  • Dealing with unsatisfied customers is difficult: they want serious consideration and efficient processing without sending reminders
  • Complaints managers must clarify expectations (often poorly expressed)
  • Not minimizing consequences while protecting the company's image

7.3 Complaints as an Imperfect Indicator

Critical warning: Not all dissatisfied customers complain. The majority (approximately 95%) do NOT express their opinion spontaneously and many leave without warning. The more complaints recorded, the more customers are leaving — but the visible complaints represent only the tip of the iceberg.
