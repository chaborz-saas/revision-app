Summary — Relationship Marketing Ch5: The Dark Side of Relationship Marketing

This chapter examines the pitfalls, misunderstandings, and ethical limits of Relationship Marketing. It explores 5 common pitfalls that managers fall into and the broader limits that marketers and consumers face.

1. Pitfall 1: CRM = IT? 2. Pitfall 2: Compensate quality? 3. Pitfall 3: Top-down? 4. Pitfall 4: Immediate sales? 5. Pitfall 5: Stable? 6. Limits of RM 7. Personalization Paradox

Pitfall 1: CRM is Just Information Systems and IT?

FALSE. Many managers think they are doing CRM because they committed to large CRM software (Salesforce, SAP, etc.).
Reality: Technology is only a support to strategy. The strategy must define:
  • What is at stake
  • The objectives
  • The kind of actions to undertake
  • The tools to consider
The marketing department should specify what the CRM Information System should do. IT implements, but does not drive the strategy.

Pitfall 2: RM Should Compensate for Lack of Product Quality?

FALSE. Relationship Marketing is useless if the basic offer (product or service) is not of sufficient quality.
The cake analogy: Basic quality = the cake. Relationship Marketing = only the icing on the cake. Without a good cake, the icing is meaningless.
Counter-productive effect: Consumers are irritated when repeatedly solicited while the basic contract is not fulfilled. However, RM can sometimes be used to overcome or pardon occasional dissatisfaction (especially true in services).

Pitfall 3: RM Policy is Top-Down and One-Sided (Brand → Customer)?

FALSE. Many relationship programs operate on the principle: "My dear customer, I want to have a strong relationship with you." But customers also have something to say!
Reality:
  • If the company does not listen carefully (comments, claims, suggestions), customer involvement will remain limited
  • Relationship policies based only on control and influence do not work well
  • This is even more true since the advent of social media
Principle: Don't just talk to customers — learn from them!

Pitfall 4: The Strategic Objective of RM is to Stimulate Immediate Sales?

FALSE. Many relationship programs are nothing more than disguised sales promotions with a single goal: promote new products and boost monthly sales (bonus effect).
The two dimensions of a relationship program:
  1. Short-term: Boost immediate sales (promotional campaigns) — but sales drop immediately when promotions stop
  2. Long-term: Build and sustain cognitive and emotional connections with the brand — generates increasing income over time
The challenge: Managing Customer Relationship is finding the right balance between short-term and long-term revenue.

Pitfall 5: Relationship Marketing is a Stable Activity?

FALSE. Everything in a business is fast-moving, and it is vital to stay in tune with the environment.
This is especially true because Relationship Marketing relies heavily on data, which is a constantly evolving field. Technologies, consumer behaviors, regulations (e.g., GDPR), and competitive landscapes all change rapidly. RM strategies must be continuously adapted.

6. The Limits of Relationship Marketing

6.1 Ethical Questions

Key ethical questions that marketers should ask themselves:
  • Which RM practices would you personally consider unfair?
  • What ethical questions does the use of AI in RM raise?
  • Is RM beneficial for everyone? For all types of companies, industries, and products?

6.2 Consumer Perception Issues

Negative perceptions consumers may have:
  • Intrusion: Feeling of being over-solicited, invaded by marketing communications
  • Privacy concerns: Discomfort with data collection and use
  • Manipulation: Feeling that personalization is used to manipulate purchasing decisions
  • Inauthenticity: Perceiving "relationships" as insincere, purely profit-driven

6.3 Consumer Gaming Strategies

Some consumers take advantage of RM policies while giving little back:
  • Cherry-picking: Only responding to promotions and discounts, never buying at full price
  • Program hopping: Joining multiple loyalty programs without genuine loyalty to any brand
  • Gaming rewards: Exploiting program rules to maximize rewards with minimum spending
  • Serial switching: Constantly switching to capture new-customer offers

7. The Personalization Paradox & GDPR

7.1 Paradoxical Perception of Personalization

The paradox: Consumers simultaneously want personalized experiences AND are uncomfortable with the data collection required to deliver them. They appreciate relevant recommendations but feel uneasy about how much companies know about them.

7.2 GDPR is Not Perceived as Protection

Despite GDPR (General Data Protection Regulation) being designed to protect consumers, surveys show that consumers do not necessarily perceive it as effective protection. Many feel:
  • GDPR consent forms are confusing and tedious
  • They have little real control over their data
  • Companies still find ways to collect and use data
  • The regulation has not fundamentally changed their experience
