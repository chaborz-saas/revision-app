Summary — SEBS Ch 1: Climate & Energy Fundamentals

1. Course Framework & Objectives

This course explains the physical framework in which societies and the economy evolve. It introduces the concept of transition (ecological, energy, societal) and provides key figures and orders of magnitude. The overarching aim is to design credible innovation strategies based on physical constraints, not only financial or accounting ones.

Key idea: Sustainable development change is not only about carbon emissions or climate change. It is systemic and touches all dimensions of human activity.

2. Sustainable Development (SD)

Sustainable Development: Development that aims to meet the needs of current generations without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs. It takes into account economic, social, and environmental considerations that are mutually reinforcing. (European Union, 2008)

Two Central Notions

  1. Economic Development: All the positive changes a society can experience — improvements in living conditions, health, and education. Distinct from the simple notion of growth.
  2. Sustainability: An intergenerational view of development. Understanding the consequences of our actions at 25, 50, 75 years into the future.

The 3 Pillars of Sustainable Development

SocialInclusion, population healthEquitable (with Economy): inequalities & solidarities
EconomyProductive capacity, innovation, research, integration into globalisationViable (with Environment): production & consumption patterns

Who Is Concerned?

Citizens, Companies, and Local/regional authorities. SD operates at both micro level (competitive advantage, profit, differentiation, HR/Marketing/Production) and macro level (overall strategy, cities, mobility, water, energy, climate change).

The 17 Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs)

Adopted by the United Nations, the 17 SDGs provide a universal framework for addressing global challenges including poverty, inequality, climate change, environmental degradation, peace, and justice.

3. The Anthropocene

Anthropocene: A proposed geological epoch highlighting the period in which human activity has become the dominant influence on the planet's climate, ecosystems, and geology. From Greek: "anthropos" (human) + "kainos" (new/recent). Popularized by Nobel Prize-winning scientist Paul Crutzen in the early 2000s.

Why It Matters

  • Marks a shift from the Holocene (which began about 11,700 years ago after the last Ice Age).
  • Human actions now rival natural forces in shaping the Earth.

Human Impact Markers

  • Climate change (greenhouse gases, global warming)
  • Loss of biodiversity (mass extinctions)
  • Pollution (plastics, chemicals, nuclear fallout)
  • Land use changes (deforestation, urbanization, agriculture)
  • Geological traces (concrete, plastics, industrial residues)
The Great Acceleration: Huge technical and scientific progress has been accompanied by a "levy" type removal of planet resources, massive use of all energy sources, impact on all earth systems, and transformation of biosphere metabolism.

4. Understanding the World as a System

System: A set containing elements where each element has properties and elements interact according to rules. The world is a complex system of interconnected sub-systems.

The Lake, the Fish and the Fishermen (Systems Thinking)

This parable illustrates how human exploitation and natural regeneration interact:

  • Fish populations renew themselves at an optimal level — too few or too many fish reduces renewal.
  • With more boats and technological advances, fishing pressure increases rapidly, often surpassing the lake's natural renewal capacity.

Three Possible Evolutions

ScenarioOutcome
1. StabilizationFish stocks decline but stabilize at a low level; boat numbers stabilize too.
2. OscillationFish and boat numbers fluctuate in cycles — unstable but no collapse.
3. CollapseOverfishing leads to the collapse of both fish stocks and the fishing fleet.
Key lesson: Sustainability depends on maintaining balance between human exploitation and natural regeneration.

Our Human Sub-System

Human society is a "big processing machine" — extracting resources, transforming them, and producing waste. A fundamental problem in our historical "progress measurement" indicators is that the economy "on the books" (value creation as traditionally measured) only equals the reality of value creation if resources are available in infinite quantity.

Circular Economy Sankey Diagram: Shows how natural resources flow through the global economy. Left: extraction of minerals, ores, fossil fuels, biomass. Middle: transformation into products/services (housing, mobility, food). Right: after use, most become waste (67.4 Gt) while only 9.3 Gt is reused/recycled. Underlines the need for a more circular economy.

7. Understanding Climate Change

The Climate System

The climate system is composed of 5 interacting components: the atmosphere, hydrosphere, cryosphere, biosphere, and continental surfaces. Human activities now interact with all of these.

Things to Remember about Climate Change:
  • Climate should not be confused with weather — different time scales and study methods.
  • The climate system is complex and constantly changing.
  • The greenhouse effect is a natural process, reinforced by human emissions.
  • Climate is very sensitive to temperature: small changes have big consequences.
  • The situation is unprecedented essentially in the speed of change.

IPCC Key Messages (AR5)

  1. Human activities are responsible for accelerating climate change.
  2. It is important to limit the temperature increase to +2 degrees C before the end of the century.
  3. Beyond that, we run the risk of the climate machine running out of control.

8. Energy and Its Availability

What Is Energy?

Energy: Allows you to modify speed, temperature, shape, chemical composition, altitude, light, etc. Energy measures the transformations of the world. Energy consumption = the rate at which the world is changing.

Power vs. Energy

Power is measured in kilowatts (kW) — analogous to the speed of a cyclist.
Energy is measured in kilowatt-hours (kWh) — analogous to the distance traveled.
Energy (kWh) = Power (kW) x Time (h)

The Concept of "Energy Slaves"

Primary energy expenditure per person can be expressed in "slave equivalent." A person in a developed country (2000) uses the equivalent of many dozens of "energy slaves" compared to a prehistoric hunter-gatherer. A human body generates only about 2.3 kWh/day.

Global Energy Mix

Unshared domination of fossil fuels: Despite the growth of renewables, the global energy mix remains overwhelmingly dominated by fossil fuels. The mix evolves slowly, and population has grown 6x while energy use has grown 30x.

The Carbon Double Constraint

Carbon Double Constraint: Fossil fuels face a double challenge:
  1. Scarcity — limited reserves, peak oil concept
  2. GHG emissions — contributing to climate change

Challenges with Renewable Energy

  • Intermittency is THE main problem — wind and solar are variable and not yet storable at scale.
  • Concentration/energy density — renewables generally have lower energy density than fossil fuels.
  • Solar radiation on the ground equals 10,000 times human consumption, but capture and conversion remain challenges.

9. Key Conclusions from Sessions 1 & 2

Summary of the energy challenge:
  • Fossil fuels will become scarcer.
  • Renewable energies will not solve all problems alone.
  • Energy savings are mandatory.
  • The priority order is: Sobriety → Energy Efficiency → Renewable Energy.
  • The society of "always more" is no longer possible; it is desirable to move towards "always better."
  • Fight against precariousness and social crises, access to energy for all.
