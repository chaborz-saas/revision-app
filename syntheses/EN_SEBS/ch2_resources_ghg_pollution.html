Summary — SEBS Ch 2: Mineral Resources, GHG & Pollution

Part 5: Access to Mineral Resources

The Human Processing Machine

As established in Sessions 1-2, our human society functions as a massive "processing machine." It extracts resources from nature, transforms them into products and services, and generates waste. A critical finding is that human-made products now outweigh living organisms on Earth, highlighting the unprecedented scale of material extraction.

Mining Trends

Key trend: The same upward trend applies to all resources, including metals. Mining continues at an accelerating pace, and even sand is under pressure from global demand (construction, glass, electronics).

Critical Metals

Critical metals: Metals that are essential for modern technology (electronics, renewable energy, defense) but face supply risks due to geological scarcity, geopolitical concentration, or lack of substitutes. Examples include rare earth elements, cobalt, lithium, and tungsten.
  • Geopolitics plays a major role: many critical metals are concentrated in a few countries (e.g., China for rare earths, DRC for cobalt).
  • Huge demand ahead due to the energy transition (batteries, solar panels, wind turbines, electric vehicles).

Metal Recycling Rates

According to the UNEP (2011), recycling rates for most metals are alarmingly low:

Concern: Many of the metals most critical to the energy transition (lithium, rare earths, cobalt) have very low recycling rates, making supply chains vulnerable.

Part 6: Greenhouse Gas (GHG) Pollution

The Greenhouse Effect

Greenhouse effect: A natural process by which certain gases in the atmosphere trap some of the sun's radiation reflected by the Earth, warming the planet. Without it, Earth would be frozen at -18 degrees C. Human emissions are reinforcing this natural effect.

How It Works

  1. The atmosphere scatters some solar radiation but transmits a large proportion to Earth.
  2. The Earth heats up and emits radiation back toward space.
  3. Greenhouse gases in the atmosphere reflect this radiation back to Earth, causing warming.

Two critical takeaways:
  1. Current global warming could reach +5 degrees C in a few hundred years.
  2. The problem is the unprecedented speed of change (almost 100 times faster than natural changes). The question is whether ecosystems can keep up.

Already +1.4 degrees C

The recession of glaciers is already visible (e.g., the "Mer de Glace" in the Alps has receded dramatically since 1823). Many tipping points are related to global warming, and we are approaching several of them.

CO2 as the First Cause of Global Warming

CO2 is the primary greenhouse gas emitted by human activities. Its historical accumulation in the atmosphere is the main driver of current warming. The number of people affected by climate impacts is growing rapidly.

IPCC Assessment Report 6 (AR6) Scenarios

The IPCC models various scenarios ranging from optimistic (strong emission reductions) to catastrophic (business-as-usual). All scenarios require significant changes, and the window for limiting warming to +1.5 degrees C is rapidly closing.

Part 7: Other Pollutions

Eutrophication

Eutrophication: Excessive enrichment of water bodies with nutrients (especially nitrogen and phosphorus), leading to algal blooms, oxygen depletion, and ecosystem damage. Primarily caused by agricultural runoff and wastewater.

Pollution and Health

Pollution significantly increases mortality, with 90% of pollution-related deaths occurring in developing countries. Water quality is deteriorating globally, affecting billions of people.

Plastic Pollution

Scale of the problem: Plastic pollution is a global crisis. Rivers are the main conduits for plastic entering oceans. Plastics never fully degrade in nature — they break down into microplastics that contaminate ecosystems and food chains.

Part 8: Resources from Living Things

Biodiversity Loss

  • Human impact on animal sub-systems is severe and accelerating.
  • We are witnessing a clear acceleration in species extinction — the 6th mass extinction.

Drivers of Current Extinction

Main drivers: Habitat destruction, overexploitation, pollution, invasive species, and climate change. Pressure on terrestrial environments continues to grow.

Carbon Footprint of Food

Food production is a major contributor to greenhouse gas emissions. The carbon footprint varies enormously by food type, with animal products (especially beef) having the highest footprint.

Part 9: Remaining Challenges for Sustainability

Dimensions for Action

Sustainable development cannot be limited to environmental issues alone. It is systemic and requires action on all dimensions of human activity:

DimensionElements
Availability & UseEnergy sources, metals, mineral resources, water, agricultural land, marine space
Human CapabilitiesDemographics, health, governance, mobilization, social bonding, resilience
Efficiency of TransformationStrategic vs. redundant activities, technology choices, externalities, pollution levels
Value SystemSubjective value (fashion, marketing), objective value (accounting), unit of value (monetary system)

The Jevons Paradox (Rebound Effect)

Jevons Paradox: When technological progress increases the efficiency of resource use, the resulting savings may lead to increased consumption rather than decreased consumption, because the resource becomes cheaper or more attractive to use.
Example: More fuel-efficient cars might encourage people to drive more, offsetting the efficiency gains. This is why the debate on "decoupling CO2 emissions from GDP" is complex.

Additional Trends

  • Demography: Continued population growth increases pressure on all resources.
  • Extreme poverty: Over a billion people still live in extreme poverty.
  • Interconnected risks: Environmental, social, and economic risks reinforce each other.

The Circular Economy

Circular Economy: A holistic approach aimed at eliminating waste by keeping resources in circulation for as long as possible. A self-regenerative economy where outputs become inputs for new processes.

Linear vs. Circular Economy

AspectLinear EconomyCircular Economy
ModelExtract, Manufacture, Use, DisposeRecover and Regenerate at all lifecycle stages
MindsetMass consumptionOptimal use
WasteGenerated as a by-productDesigned out of the system

Fundamental Principles: Reduce, Reuse, Recycle

Reduce: Minimize use of natural resources and waste generation. Design for efficiency and durability. Minimize environmental impacts from the design stage.
Reuse: Extend the useful life of products through repair, refurbishment, and repurposing. Promote sharing, repairing, and refurbishing programs. Return systems and reuse programs.
Recycle: Convert waste into resources for new products. Develop innovative recycling infrastructures. Use recyclable and easily disassemblable materials.

Key Conclusions from Session 3

The two major challenges are:
  1. The carbon double constraint (fossil fuel scarcity + GHG emissions).
  2. The systemic nature of sustainability — it requires action across all dimensions (resources, human capabilities, efficiency, value systems).

SD is not limited to environmental issues. It is systemic and requires action on all dimensions. The circular economy offers a framework for transforming our "processing machine" from a linear to a regenerative model.
