Part 10: Defining Innovation — Real Innovation Is About Business Models

What Is Innovation?

Innovation is not limited to technical invention. The course emphasizes that competitiveness = business model innovation. Real innovation that works requires boldness to change business models, not merely improve products or processes.

Four Traditional Categories of Innovation

Four categories used to study how companies evolve and create value:
  1. Product Innovation: Developing new or improved goods and services.
  2. Process Innovation: Improving how products are made or delivered (e.g., Toyota's Lean Production System).
  3. Organizational Innovation: Changing internal structures, management methods, or workflows (e.g., Google's flat management structure).
  4. Market Innovation: Finding new markets or changing how products are positioned and sold (e.g., Netflix shifting to online streaming).
Key insight: These categories are interconnected. Progress in one area (e.g., process innovation) often supports or enables innovation in others (e.g., product or market innovation). However, the real competitive advantage comes from business model innovation — not just improving within existing categories.

Business Model Innovation vs. Technical Invention

Major innovations often come from new business strategies, not just technical inventions. Changing a business model requires boldness and a willingness to rethink fundamental assumptions about how value is created and delivered.

BlaBlaCar: The technology (website + geolocation) was not new. The real innovation was the business model of carpooling between individuals — connecting drivers and passengers to share travel costs on long-distance trips. It created a new collaborative mobility market, changing how millions of people travel affordably.
Decathlon: Sports equipment already existed. The innovation came from vertical integration — designing, producing, and selling its own brands (Quechua, Domyos, etc.) under one roof. This strategy reduced prices, democratized access to sport, and made Decathlon a global leader in affordable innovation.

Part 11: "Reinventing Innovation"

What Digital Technologies Really Change

Prosumption (or prosumerisation): The concept that people are both producers and consumers at the same time. Thanks to digital tools (social media, 3D printing, YouTube, e-commerce platforms), the means of creation, production, and distribution are now accessible to everyone.

Digital technologies transform the roles of consumers and producers:

  • Consumers can create, produce, and share their own content or products.
  • Companies and individuals collaborate in value creation.
  • The means of creation, production, and distribution are now accessible to everyone.
Vinted — second-hand fashion: Vinted has become the leading clothing seller in France (by volume). Second-hand now represents 10.9% of total clothing purchases by value and 16.3% among 18-34 year-olds. In other words, roughly one in ten garments purchased in France is second-hand, and among young adults, nearly one in six. This reflects a massive shift in consumer behavior.

The "3rd Wave" (Beyond Toffler)

Alvin Toffler's Three Waves of Civilization:
  • 1st Wave — Agricultural Age: Based on land and manual labor.
  • 2nd Wave — Industrial Age: Based on machines, factories, and mass production.
  • 3rd Wave — Digital/Information Age: Based on information and social capital.

Key Characteristics of the 3rd Wave

Dimension3rd Wave Characteristics
Key ResourceInformation and social capital (connections, data, trust)
Key OrganisationPlatforms (Uber, Airbnb, YouTube)
Family & SocietyMore diverse and network-based
RolesProsumers — people who both produce and consume (YouTubers, Etsy sellers)
ProductionCustomized instead of mass-produced
EconomyMore decentralized, connected through digital networks
The 3rd Wave represents a shift from industrial mass production to digital collaboration and personalization.

Platforms and Social Capital

In this new paradigm, social capital is key. New business models require embarking all players around a shared vision. This is all the more important when the goal is to transform society itself toward sustainability.

Platform economy: Platforms (like Uber, Airbnb, Vinted) do not own the means of production but connect supply and demand through digital infrastructure. They create value through network effects — the more users, the more valuable the platform becomes.

Case Study: Circular Economy in Action

SOJO (sojo.uk)

An innovative service focused on clothing repairs and alterations, connecting consumers with local tailors through an app. It extends the lifespan of garments and reduces textile waste, exemplifying the circular economy principle of "reuse."

Wrote Farm (wrotefarm.com)

An example of sustainable agriculture innovation, demonstrating how new business models can regenerate local ecosystems while creating economic value.

Key Takeaways from Session 4

Core messages:
  • Real innovation = business model innovation, not just technical invention.
  • The 4 traditional categories (product, process, organizational, market) are interconnected, but business model change is what drives competitiveness.
  • Digital technologies enable prosumption and platform-based value creation.
  • The 3rd Wave shifts from mass production to personalization and collaboration.
  • Social capital and platform dynamics are the new competitive foundations.
  • Major innovations often come from business strategy (BlaBlaCar, Decathlon), not from new technology alone.
