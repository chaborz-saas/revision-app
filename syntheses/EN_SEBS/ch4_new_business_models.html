Summary — SEBS Ch 4: New Business Models for a Sustainable World

Overview: New Technologies and Business Models

Session 5 explores the specific sectors and strategies where new technologies and business models can have maximum impact for sustainability. The goals are to:

  • Stabilize climate
  • Rethink our energy system
  • Rethink the inputs and outputs of our "processing machine"
  • Regenerate the biosphere
  • Revisit how we feed humanity
  • Educate for new societal models

Part 12: Stabilize Climate

Reducing GHG Sources

Based on IPCC data, achieving net-zero greenhouse gas emissions requires action across multiple sectors simultaneously:

  • Direct emissions come from buildings, industry, and transport.
  • Indirect emissions are mainly linked to energy supply and use.
  • The energy sector provides the largest share of total reductions, both direct and indirect.
  • LULUCF (Land Use, Land-Use Change and Forestry) acts as a carbon sink, helping absorb remaining emissions.
Key insight: Achieving net zero requires strong reductions across ALL sectors plus additional CO2 removal from land and nature-based solutions.

Innovation in Agriculture

  • Breed selection: Developing livestock breeds that produce less methane.
  • Food additives: Supplements that reduce methane emissions from ruminants.
  • Rice production: Changing rice cultivation methods to reduce methane from flooded paddies.

Transportation: The ASI Framework

ASI Framework for Sustainable Transportation:
  • A — Avoid: Reduce the need for transport (remote work, local production).
  • S — Shift: Move to less carbon-intensive modes (public transport, cycling, rail).
  • I — Improve: Make remaining transport more efficient (electric vehicles, better engines).

Construction Innovations

  • Build differently: Fundamentally rethink construction practices.
  • Rock that captures CO2: Using materials that absorb carbon dioxide during their lifecycle.
  • Wood-based concrete: Replacing traditional concrete with wood-based alternatives that store carbon.

Biochar

Biochar: A charcoal-like substance produced by burning biomass at high temperature with limited oxygen. It offers three benefits:
  1. Stable form of carbon — locks carbon in soil for centuries.
  2. Clean source of energy — the production process generates usable energy.
  3. Improvement of soil quality — enhances fertility, water retention, and microbial activity.

Part 13: Rethink Our Energy System

Main Challenges

The current energy system faces the carbon double constraint (scarcity + GHG). The solution rests on two pillars:

  1. De-carbonated electricity — clean power generation.
  2. New molecules — especially decarbonated hydrogen.

Key Technologies

Truck Electrification: Heavy transport is moving toward electrification, a major challenge given the energy density requirements.
Agrivoltaism: Combining agricultural production with solar energy generation on the same land. French nuclear power production is equivalent to agrivoltaism applied to just 2% of French cultivated area.
Gen IV Nuclear Reactors: Next-generation nuclear technologies including breeder reactors (which generate more fuel than they consume) and thorium-based reactors (more abundant fuel, less waste).
Aquifer Thermal Energy Storage (ATES): Storing heat or cold in underground aquifers for later use — an efficient way to manage seasonal energy demands.

The Rise of Decarbonated Hydrogen

Hydrogen as an energy carrier: Hydrogen represents a full rethink of energy management. It offers multiple supply pathways (electrolysis from renewables, steam reformation, methane cracking), multiple storage and transport options (gas networks, dedicated storage, export markets), and multiple uses (fuel cells for transport, industrial processes, heating, electricity grid balancing).
Hydrogen ecosystem: Connects renewable energies, electricity grids, gas networks, industrial processes, and transport solutions into an integrated energy system. Electrolysis from renewable sources produces "green hydrogen" — the most desirable form.

Part 14: Reduce Consumption of Resources

From Ownership to Service Functionality

Economy of Service Functionality: Instead of selling products, companies sell the service the product provides. This shifts incentives toward durability, repairability, and efficiency, since the provider retains ownership and bears maintenance costs.
Energy Performance Contract (concept since 1937): An early example of the service economy model in energy management — paying for the result (energy savings) rather than the equipment.

New Materials and Processes

  • Resilient materials: New deformable and resilient materials that resist breakage.
  • "Living" material: Innovative materials inspired by biological processes.
  • Easier degrading materials: Bio-based alternatives from cactus, grape, mushroom, pineapple, cork, eucalyptus, hemp, oyster shells, potatoes, and more.
  • Upcycling: Extending product use by transforming waste into higher-value products.
  • Bioplastic development: Replacing petroleum-based plastics with bio-based alternatives.
Energy transition metals: The energy transition requires huge quantities of metals (for batteries, solar panels, wind turbines). This creates new resource challenges even as we solve climate challenges.

Part 15: Regenerate the Biosphere

Bioremediation: Using living organisms (bacteria, fungi, plants) to clean up contaminated environments. This includes removing pollutants from soil, water, and air through natural biological processes.

Part 16: Revisit How We Feed Humanity

Key Innovations

  • Valorize and transform new resources: Finding value in waste streams and underutilized biological resources.
  • Agriculture 4.0: Using digital technologies, sensors, drones, AI, and precision farming to optimize agricultural production while minimizing environmental impact.
  • Proteins out of thin air: Emerging technologies that produce protein from CO2, water, and electricity using microorganisms — potentially revolutionizing food production with minimal land and water use.

Part 17: Educate for New Models

  • Continuous learning: The transition requires ongoing education for workers, managers, and citizens.
  • Knowledge exchange: Sharing best practices, innovations, and solutions across sectors and borders.

Key Takeaways from Session 5

The "New Abundance" concept (BCG): Resource constraints should be seen as strategic opportunities. Companies that ignore physical constraints (resources, energy, climate, raw materials) face existential risks. Those that adapt their business models to work within these constraints can gain significant competitive advantages.
  • A sustainable strategy must account for physical constraints, not just financial ones.
  • Innovation must span all sectors: climate, energy, resources, biosphere, food, education.
  • The shift from ownership to service functionality is a fundamental business model transformation.
  • Hydrogen represents a systemic rethink of energy management.
