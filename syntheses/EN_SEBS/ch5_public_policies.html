Part 18: Policies Clarifying Goals

First Focus: Climate Change

Climate change has been the primary focus of international public policy efforts, starting with the 2015 Paris Agreement pledges.

Carbon Budgets

Carbon Budget: The maximum total amount of CO2 that can be emitted while still keeping global warming below a certain threshold (e.g., +1.5 degrees C or +2 degrees C). Once the budget is exhausted, emissions must reach zero to prevent further warming.

Key principle: The later we start reducing emissions, the faster and more drastically we must cut them. Delay makes the transition exponentially harder.

The International Energy Agency (IEA) models three energy futures for 2040:

Net-Zero by 2050 (IEA Revised Target)

  • Total energy supply falls by 7% between 2020 and 2030 in the Net Zero Emissions (NZE) scenario.
  • Solar PV and wind become the leading sources of electricity globally before 2030.
  • Together they provide nearly 70% of global generation in 2050.
The big question: Is this achievable? Many countries have made net-zero pledges, but the gap between pledges and action remains significant.

Part 19: Flushing Out Greenwashing on "Carbon Neutrality"

Carbon Neutral vs. Net Zero

Carbon Neutral: A company can offset its emissions by funding external projects (planting trees, carbon credits). It does not require reducing all emissions inside the company. Focus is mainly on compensation, not deep transformation.
Net Zero: Requires major reductions of emissions across the entire value chain (Scope 1, 2, and 3). Only a small remaining part can be offset. Aligned with science-based climate pathways (1.5 degrees C). Much more ambitious than carbon neutrality.

Scope 1, 2, and 3 Emissions

GHG Protocol — Three Scopes:
  • Scope 1 — Direct emissions: From sources owned or controlled by the company (factories, vehicles, boilers).
  • Scope 2 — Indirect energy emissions: From purchased electricity, heating, and cooling.
  • Scope 3 — Other indirect emissions: Everything else in the value chain (employee commuting, supply chain, product use, waste disposal, non-energy purchases).
The GHG Protocol is the international standard that maps greenhouse gas emission sources into about twenty categories grouped into these three scopes. Scope 3 typically represents the largest share of a company's total emissions.

Countries Underestimating Their Emissions

A key area of controversy: many countries attempt to offset fossil fuel emissions by claiming carbon absorption by land within their borders. UN rules allow countries to subtract significant amounts of emissions this way.

Malaysia example: Malaysia's climate report claimed its trees absorb carbon four times faster than similar forests in neighboring Indonesia, allowing the country to subtract over 243 million tons of CO2 — slashing 73% of emissions from its bottom line. An estimated 25% of global emissions may be "missing" due to such accounting practices.

The Concept of "Absolute Zero"

Absolute Zero: Reaching zero emissions with no reliance on CO2 removal technologies. No offsets, no carbon capture — just zero emissions produced. This is the most stringent target, contrasting with both "carbon neutral" (which allows compensation) and "net zero" (which allows minimal offsetting).

Part 20: Europe Trying to Take the Lead

The European Green Deal

The EU's comprehensive strategy for achieving climate neutrality, with key milestones:

  • Climate target: 55% reduction in GHG by 2030, then net zero by 2050.
  • Sectoral constraints: e.g., end of production of new combustion engine cars by 2035.
  • CAP reform: Common Agricultural Policy reform to align with sustainability goals.

The EU Taxonomy

EU Taxonomy: A unified framework establishing which economic activities can be considered environmentally sustainable. It boosts green investment, increases transparency, and aligns financial flows with climate goals.

  2. Climate Change Adaptation
  3. Water and Marine Resources Protection
  4. Circular Economy Promotion
  5. Pollution Prevention
  6. Biodiversity and Ecosystem Restoration

CSRD — Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive

Double Materiality (key CSRD concept): Companies must report sustainability issues from two perspectives:
  1. Financial Materiality (Outside → Inside): How environmental/social issues affect the company's financial performance (climate risks, supply chain disruptions, regulations, reputational risks).
  2. Impact Materiality (Inside → Outside): How the company's activities impact people and the environment (carbon emissions, pollution, human rights).
Double materiality = "impact on the company" + "impact of the company." It is one of the core innovations of CSRD.

ESRS (European Sustainability Reporting Standards)

The CSRD is implemented through the ESRS norms, which provide detailed reporting standards. Sector-specific ESRS are still being developed.

2025 Omnibus: CSRD Taking a Break?

Important recent development: The 2025 Omnibus proposal suggests significant changes:
  • A drastic reduction in the number of companies concerned by CSRD.
  • Big simplifications of reporting requirements ahead.
  • CSDDD (Corporate Sustainability Due Diligence Directive) also impacted.
This reflects political pushback and concerns about regulatory burden on businesses, creating uncertainty about the pace and scope of EU sustainability regulation.

International Context

The EU regulatory framework exists within a broader international context:

  • Paris Agreement (2015): Climate change commitments.
  • SDGs (2012-2015): Sustainable Development Goals.
  • Kunming-Montreal (2022): Biodiversity agreement.
  • International standards: GRI (Global Reporting Initiative), TCFD/ISSB (climate financial disclosures), TNFD (nature-related disclosures), SBTi (Science Based Targets initiative), SBTN (Science Based Targets for Nature).

Key Takeaways from Session 6

Core messages:
  • Public policies are essential but face enormous implementation challenges.
  • The distinction between "carbon neutral," "net zero," and "absolute zero" is critical for avoiding greenwashing.
  • Scope 1/2/3 accounting reveals the true breadth of a company's climate impact.
  • The EU is attempting to lead with the Green Deal, Taxonomy, CSRD, and CSDDD — but the 2025 Omnibus suggests pushback.
  • Double materiality (CSRD) represents a paradigm shift in how companies report sustainability.
  • Delay in climate action makes the transition exponentially harder.
  • The gap between climate pledges and actual action remains significant worldwide.
